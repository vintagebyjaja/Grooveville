# Introduction

At a meeting about the future of a public park, the people most practiced at attending meetings usually know where to sit. They have seen the agenda. They know how long public comment lasts and which item will come up after half the room has gone home. The teenager who uses the basketball court every afternoon may know things about that park that no one on the planning staff knows. He may know which light goes out first, who brings a broom when broken glass turns up, and why a girl with a good jump shot stopped coming on Saturdays. He can know all this and still miss the meeting. He might be working. He might never have heard about it. His mother might have seen the notice and thought public comment was something that happened after the decision.

What would it take for the people making the decision to hear him? Part of the answer belongs to him. He may have to find somebody who has sat through a meeting, figure out what he wants to say, and get his name on the list. A good deal of the answer belongs to the people holding the meeting. They chose the hour, the room, the language on the notice and the method for getting it out. If they keep hearing from the same six people, they have work to do. The boy can learn the rules and the rules can still deserve changing. Both things can be true before he says a word.

That park is an imagined example. Its question runs through this book. How do you learn to move through the world as it is while helping make it a better place to live?

By groove, this book means the way a person learns to move through life with intention. It includes how you read a room, how you keep your word and how you recognize when the way you learned to survive is costing you something. It has room for the structure of a day and the surprise of a new friendship. It asks how you know when to let a thing go, when to risk being laughed at, and when to make an opening for somebody coming behind you. Finding your own way through those decisions takes practice. Having people who will tell you the truth makes a difference.

A book of laws can sound as if someone is about to hand you a list of reasons your life went wrong. These laws are principles for making decisions. Their worth comes from how well they help you think in a specific situation. The law of Time looks different to someone whose supervisor changes the schedule every Thursday than it does to someone who decides when Thursday's meeting starts. Both people can learn to value time. One of them can return a measure of it to the other by posting a dependable schedule. An honest account of responsibility must ask who had the power to make a choice.

The perspective here is rooted in Black American life. That means paying attention to the ordinary breadth of it. A neighborhood has people who can tell you which house to run to for help and people who need a place to run. It has nurses, musicians, shop owners, transit workers, people between jobs, and people who never want to run a business. The person who calls the whole block family may disagree with her actual family about almost everything. There are elders who can explain how they bought the first house in the family and young people who may spend a decade trying to persuade a landlord to treat a rental application fairly. There are Black Americans whose families have worked the same stretch of soil for generations and others who arrived here recently and learned a new map of race after moving. These lives belong in the same conversation without being compressed into a single account.

Racism belongs in that conversation because it shapes actual choices. A lender, a hiring manager, an administrator and a teacher can each make a decision that someone else has to live with. A family can take care to save money and still feel the effects of generations of unequal access to property and credit. A person can be excellent at her job and discover that excellence gets described differently depending on who demonstrates it. The chapters ask what a reader can do with the power available to them, including what someone can do when the decision is theirs to make. In some situations the best move is a personal habit. In others it is a policy change, a public record, a group of people willing to ask the same question or a different person at the table when the decision is made.

This book starts with Reflection because the ability to observe yourself changes what you do with the others. A person can learn to scout an institution and still mistake every closed door for a judgment about their worth. A person can learn to protect their time and use the new discipline to avoid everyone who might need them. Reflection asks you to pay attention to what you assumed and what your actions made harder for someone else. Phone Down takes that attention out into the world. There are conversations where the smallest meaningful act is to hear the whole sentence. Scout moves from the immediate surroundings to the people and institutions that set the terms of an opportunity. Branding asks what happens when a reputation meets a stereotype, and what parts of your character you can show over time.

The next set of laws puts those ideas to work in relationships. I Say = I Will treats a promise as something another person may build their day around. Peers Aren't Dears considers what lies beneath the laugh after a degrading joke, and what adults owe the friends who trust them with private information. Endowment widens the idea of inheritance. Some people receive property. Others receive the phone number of a retired mechanic who will show them how to ask the right question before buying a car. A great many people receive something valuable alongside obligations and losses. The point is to understand what is in your care, what was withheld from others and what you can put within reach.

Risky Rewards and Disengagement can sit side by side because judgment requires knowing what you stand to lose. A person can spend years wishing they had asked for an opportunity that was never going to appear by itself. Another person can spend an afternoon defending their pride to somebody who delights in setting the terms of the argument. Strategy asks what can change if you look farther ahead than either decision. Time asks whose hours you spend and how you handle the ones you have. Planning follows the chain from a missed detail to the person who has to cover the consequences. In Mother's Child, the question gets personal again. Who are you when there is nobody in the room who is impressed by your title? The book closes with Mental Awareness because a person can build a life that looks steady from the street and still need somewhere safe to tell the truth.

The chapters include clearly identified fictional scenes and discussions grounded in documented sources. In fiction you can stay in the room long enough to hear two people misunderstand each other and see what happens when they try again. A historical record can show which institutions had authority and which options people were denied. Each kind of writing does a different job. The practical sections are invitations to try a decision at the scale of daily life. You may have more use for the part about calling somebody back than the part about changing careers. Come back when the proportions change.

You can read the book in order, and you can carry a chapter into a conversation you already need to have. If a friend is deciding whether to leave a job, reading Risky Rewards together might reveal that the question you've been answering is whether the friend has enough nerve. The friend may need help figuring out how long health coverage lasts, or a quiet place to compare two offers, or a person who will take a call on the day the decision needs to be made. A principle has more value when it sends you back to the circumstances. Two people who read the same chapter might choose differently and be able to explain their choices better.

If you read with a group, resist the temptation to assign each law to the person who needs it most. It is easy to hold a discussion about keeping your word with the cousin who was late to arrive as the only example in the room. Give everybody a chance to talk about a situation they choose. Ask what happened before the moment that attracts everybody's attention. The person who missed the family dinner may have agreed to something impossible because the last time she said she couldn't make it, four people called to argue. There is a promise for her to consider and a way of asking for one for the rest of the family to examine.

Some parts of this book may find you with very little room to maneuver. You may be doing a job you need, caring for someone who cannot be left alone or waiting for a decision that is out of your hands. A chapter can give you language for what is happening even when it cannot give you another option. Sometimes that language helps you ask a specific person for one specific thing. A neighbor may have twenty minutes to sit with your father while you call the clinic. A coworker may know where the schedule was posted before someone changed it. A small piece of help can resolve a small problem without solving the conditions that made it hard. It is worth taking the help and keeping sight of those conditions.

Sometimes the hardest thing to recognize is the difference between a familiar cost and an acceptable one. The newest employee may assume she has to take every undesirable shift because everybody before her did. One of the old hands may have spent years thinking he had to eat lunch alone to get any peace. A closer look might reveal rules with some room in them, or a line everyone has been afraid to cross by themselves. Knowing where that line is can be an act of care. Asking why it exists can be another. You can be grateful to a person for showing you how they made it through and decide that the people who come after you should have it easier.

There is another kind of inheritance here, the instruction that arrives without its explanation. Call when you arrive. Keep a little money where you can get to it. Go see for yourself. Some advice feels excessive at fourteen and merciful at forty. Some of it needs revising. A rule offered by a person who was denied the right to make a mistake might be too narrow for your circumstances. A practice that protected your grandparents may still protect you. Asking why a rule was made can bring you closer to the person who taught it, even if you choose differently.

Imagine that the teenager at the park meeting gets his three minutes. He explains that when the light by the gate goes out, the younger kids take the longer way home. He learned this from walking his sister home after practice. The woman taking minutes asks him to repeat which gate. A neighbor stands up and gives the city the date she reported the outage. A staff member asks if the young people who use the park are willing to walk the route with the maintenance crew. None of these gestures settles the future of the park. They bring the discussion closer to the lives that depend on it. A person who speaks from what he has actually seen can give other people a place to begin.

There will be days when you are the person getting three minutes. There will also be days when you set the hour of the meeting. I hope you notice when the roles change.
