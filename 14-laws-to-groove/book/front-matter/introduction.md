# Introduction

## The 14 Laws to Groove

Nobody has life completely figured out. Not the people with money, not the people with degrees, and certainly not the people who act like they have all the answers. We all make decisions, face consequences, learn lessons, and sometimes repeat mistakes we should have learned from the first time.

But there's a difference between living life and understanding how to navigate it.

Every day, we make choices that affect our future. Some are obvious, like deciding where to work, how to spend our money, or who we allow into our lives. Others seem insignificant until we recognize their consequences. The way we speak to people, the time we waste, the promises we break, and the situations we refuse to walk away from can shape our lives more than we realize.

Then there are circumstances we don't get to choose.

We don't choose the families we're born into, the neighborhoods where we grow up, or the assumptions people make when they see us. We don't control every opportunity available to us, and we certainly don't control every system that influences our lives.

For Black Americans, those realities have long included systemic discrimination, unequal access to resources, and stereotypes that can affect how we're perceived before we've had the opportunity to introduce ourselves.

Understanding these realities isn't about making excuses. It's about recognizing the difference between what we can control, what we can influence, and what needs to change.

That's where the concept of *Groove* comes in.

To Groove is to move through life with awareness, discipline, understanding, and purpose. It means knowing who you are, recognizing what's happening around you, and making decisions that reflect the person you want to become.

It doesn't mean being perfect. It doesn't mean having money, popularity, or an impressive title. And it certainly doesn't mean pretending to be someone you're not.

The fourteen laws in this book address the everyday principles that influence how we live, from managing time and honoring commitments to choosing our company, protecting our mental well-being, and understanding the systems around us.

These lessons are rooted in community experiences, but they aren't limited to one community. Whether you're a student, parent, professional, entrepreneur, or simply someone trying to make better decisions, there's something here worth considering.

You don't have to agree with every observation in this book. In fact, I encourage you to question what you read, compare it with your own experiences, and decide how each lesson applies to your circumstances.

Because the purpose of these laws isn't to tell you exactly how to live.

**It's to give you a better understanding of the life you're already living—and the one you're capable of building.**

## How to Use the Laws

You can read these laws in order or open the book where life has already given you a question. If you are deciding whether to accept a new job, Scout can help you learn how the offer works. Risky Rewards asks what the change would cost you in a difficult month. Time asks what the schedule will require on an ordinary Tuesday. Those questions can lead to different answers. Taking them seriously may lead you to negotiate a start date or gather another piece of information before you decide.

The word law describes a principle to examine and use. Circumstances will change how it applies. Keeping your word matters, and sometimes an emergency will make it impossible to do exactly what you promised. In that case, the obligation may become giving notice, helping with a handoff or making repair. Being present usually helps a conversation, and sometimes the screen in front of you is providing the captions that let you follow it. A reader should finish a chapter able to explain where the principle is useful, where it meets a limit and what information would change their mind.

Read evidence according to what it can show. A survey measures the experiences or characteristics covered by its questions and sample. A historical record may show that an official wrote down a racial classification, while leaving the consequences of a particular decision open. A short example about a family or a work shift shows how a choice might unfold. You can challenge an example by identifying a factor it leaves out. You can challenge a claim about the world by asking how it was measured and looking at another source. That habit belongs to the reader as much as to the author.

You can work with these ideas privately or in company. A book group might choose a public decision and look up what was actually decided. A family might discuss which kinds of help they are willing to offer before a relative makes a plan that depends on them. A student might take the argument about punctuality and compare it with the scheduling control workers have in a particular industry. Agreement is optional. A good discussion gives you an account of another person's reasons that they would recognize as their own.

## Where Choice Meets Circumstance

Black American communities have developed ways to pass on information and sustain one another through formal institutions, ordinary relationships and household practices. You may recognize a warning to keep a copy of a document, a relative who knows how a municipal office works or a neighbor who insists a young person ask for a written estimate. These forms of help have histories. They can reveal which systems people had reason to distrust and which people still found a way to make those systems answer.

There are many ways to be part of that history. Family traditions, immigration and migration histories, region, work and class shape what people know and what they expect. The lesson you received may have protected somebody in a particular city or at a particular time. Ask what it was protecting. Ask whether it still gives useful guidance in the setting where you live. A person can respect an elder and adapt the instruction to new information. An elder can find out how much has changed by hearing a younger person's experience through to the end.

There is work here for the person asking for fair treatment and for the person with the power to give it. A worker can prepare a careful account before asking for an evaluation to be corrected. A manager can require clear criteria and make room for a correction before a decision becomes permanent. A renter can learn how to read a zoning notice. A government office can send that notice early enough and explain it clearly enough that residents have a chance to act. When you find a habit that works for you, consider whether you have an opportunity to change the conditions somebody else will face.

Pick a question from the start of a chapter and think about a specific situation. After reading, give yourself room to consider the closing questions before deciding what to do. If you want to write, use the reflection page for a fact you want to check, a conversation you need to revisit, or a choice that looks different to you now. One real decision will give you more to learn from than a dozen promises about the person you hope to become.
