# LAW 13 — MOTHER'S CHILD

## Understanding the Law

Mother's Child asks you to know who you are when no one in the room needs to be impressed by your title. Identity includes your history, commitments, relationships and choices. A degree, affiliation or position changes what you can do. Character shows in how you use it, including how you treat people who cannot advance your career.

## The Reality Behind It

A mother may remember the person whose reputation the outside world has only recently discovered. An aunt, grandparent, adoptive parent, teacher or old friend may have known that person, too. Some readers have a painful relationship with family or keep distance for good reason. You can take ownership of your history with people you trust and choose which details you share.

Dan McAdams and Kate McLean describe narrative identity as the internalized, evolving story people develop to connect the past with a possible future. Their review discusses associations between features of these stories and well-being. It leaves room for culture, circumstances and different ways of making meaning. [13]

## Where We Go Wrong

Pressure to fit in can make us airbrush the people and places that helped us. Pressure to remain familiar can also make real growth feel like betrayal. A new way of speaking for a particular audience may be a useful skill. Trouble begins when you claim a history you did not live or treat people from your past as embarrassing evidence.

## Applying the Law

Name three experiences that shaped a value you still practice. Write what changed since then and what you want to keep. Identify one thing you will do when your public role puts pressure on that value. You can correct a misleading introduction, share credit with a person nobody invited to the stage or admit when a title has carried you past the limits of your knowledge.

## The Community Connection

Families and communities can allow members to change without demanding access to every private part of their stories. When welcoming a young person back from college, training or a different community, ask what they learned and what they want to bring back. Give them room to ask what changed while they were away.

## The Final Word

A title may introduce you. The way you use it gives people more to go on.

## Food for Thought

What part of your story do you explain differently depending on who is listening?

How can communities affirm where people come from while leaving room for change?

How might narrative identity research account for the roles of culture and material circumstances in the stories people tell?
