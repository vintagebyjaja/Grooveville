# LAW 13 — MOTHER'S CHILD

## Opening questions

Who knew you before the titles you use today?

Which part of your upbringing still shows up in your decisions?

When do you feel most like yourself?

## Understanding the Law

Mother's Child asks you to know who you are when no one in the room needs to be impressed by your title. Identity includes your history, commitments, relationships and choices. A degree, affiliation or position changes what you can do. Character shows in how you use it, including how you treat people who cannot advance your career.

## The Reality Behind It

A mother may remember the person whose reputation the outside world has only recently discovered. An aunt, grandparent, adoptive parent, teacher or old friend may have known that person, too. Some readers have a painful relationship with family or keep distance for good reason. You can take ownership of your history with people you trust and choose which details you share.

In 2013, Dan McAdams and Kate McLean described narrative identity as the internalized, evolving story people develop to connect the past with a possible future. Their review discusses associations between features of these stories and well-being. It leaves room for culture, circumstances and different ways of making meaning.

### Recognize the Difference Between Privacy and Shame

There is no requirement to explain your family life to everybody who asks. A person can decide to leave grief, a painful relationship or details about money out of a public introduction. Privacy can make it easier to speak clearly about what does belong there. If you feel you have to conceal something that supports a value you care about, ask where the pressure comes from and what would happen if you named just enough to make the value clear.

Family accounts can disagree honestly. An older person may remember a difficult decision as an act of protection. A child may remember what it was like to live with it. Both accounts can contain useful evidence. Hearing one does not require you to abandon the other. Think about what each person was able to see, what changed afterward and which part of your life you now have responsibility for describing.

Sometimes authenticity is used to enforce somebody else's expectations. You might be told that a man who asks for help has changed, or that a woman who sets a boundary thinks herself better than the people who raised her. Consider whether the demand gives you a standard you are willing to live by. You can keep a relationship where there is room to disagree. If the demand prevents you from living in safety or dignity, you may need distance or a clearer limit.

Make your own account specific enough to revise. “I was raised to help” can describe a genuine commitment. It can also ask you to keep doing work that nobody else has agreed to share. Name which help you want to offer, the value behind it and the practices you can maintain. If an elder taught you that lesson, you may be able to discuss which parts of it they had to change.

## Where We Go Wrong

Pressure to fit in can make us airbrush the people and places that helped us. Pressure to remain familiar can also make real growth feel like betrayal. A new way of speaking for a particular audience may be a useful skill. Trouble begins when you claim a history you did not live or treat people from your past as embarrassing evidence.

### Let Your Account of Yourself Change With Your Life

A person growing into a new trade, city or role often learns another way to speak. New language can help you explain the work, make a boundary clear or meet people you would otherwise have missed. Ask what you can say better after learning it and what values you want the new skill to serve. There is room to be familiar in one setting and precise in another. What deserves attention is the moment when being taken seriously seems to require denying the parts of your life you still value.

Narrative identity gives us a way to examine the account we tell ourselves. A memory can enter the account differently once you know more about the people involved. A decision you used to call betrayal may turn out to include a hard choice about a family obligation. A success you think of as solitary may depend on an aunt who gave you a place to stay. Changing an account in response to evidence can be an expression of integrity. You still get to decide which details belong in public.

An organization can use a person's background as a signal of credibility without giving them a say in what the organization does. If you are asked to be its example of somebody from a certain place, ask what role you will have, whether you can disagree and what part of your story it wants to use. Accepting a role can create an opportunity to do useful work. Declining a role can protect your freedom to decide where you speak.

Look for a behavior you can keep steady as your public role changes. You might always give the people who did the work a chance to describe it, pay an invoice by the date you promised, or admit when you need another person's expertise. These actions give someone who never knew your upbringing a fair chance to learn what you take from it.

## Applying the Law

Name three experiences that shaped a value you still practice. Write what changed since then and what you want to keep. Identify one thing you will do when your public role puts pressure on that value. You can correct a misleading introduction, share credit with a person nobody invited to the stage or admit when a title has carried you past the limits of your knowledge.

### Decide What Your Standing Allows You to Give

A title can give you a chance to hold a door open. If you are the first person from your family in a profession, let people know which advantages it supplies and what it cost to enter. You can explain how to find a vacancy, which training served you and what money you needed before your first day. If someone wants a different kind of life, listen long enough to understand why. Passing on information gives a person more choices. Asking them to repeat your route can take some of those choices away.

Look at who helped shape your account of success. A mother who believed in you may deserve a place in it. A sibling whose wages made it possible for you to study may deserve a different kind of recognition. An adult who made life harder may also appear without being given the right to define the rest of your story. You can remember what formed you and choose which relationship to maintain. Where you do not know the full history, say that.

When you have authority, use it to reward the behavior you say you value. An adult who praises honesty can make it safer for a child to admit a mistake. A manager who values family can make a schedule that leaves time for care. A leader who values support can accept help without treating it as a loss of standing. The people around you learn from the decisions you are allowed to make. That is part of what your role passes on.

## The Community Connection

Families and communities can allow members to change without demanding access to every private part of their stories. When welcoming a young person back from college, training or a different community, ask what they learned and what they want to bring back. Give them room to ask what changed while they were away.

### What You Teach by Repairing a Mistake

You may have learned that a father or older man keeps his authority by holding his ground. Consider what a child sees when that adult corrects himself. If he criticized the child before hearing what happened, he can name what he got wrong and ask them to tell him the rest. He can make the same correction to the other people who heard the original account. The apology gives the child a practical example of how to handle authority and how to repair its misuse.

A person who had no dependable father can still decide what kind of adult they want to be. Sometimes they learn from a parent who did the work alone, a coach who kept showing up or a neighbor who took their questions seriously. If you are choosing what to carry forward from your upbringing, include the ways someone made you feel welcome and the limits you had to learn on your own. You can practice those values as a parent, an uncle, a mentor or a friend.

### Grow Without Turning Your History Into a Test

Some people return to a place that helped raise them and hear that they have changed. They may have. Education, work, grief and distance can change the habits that once made you recognizable. You can listen for the change the other person means. Perhaps they miss that you used to call. Perhaps they expect you to laugh at a remark you no longer find funny. The right response depends on which relationship or value you want to preserve.

The same question goes the other way. You may return expecting a younger relative to want the role you once played in their life. They may have learned how to make decisions without your help. Ask what support they would welcome. The ability to let somebody grow into a person you could not predict is part of the care you can offer them. It leaves room for them to ask again later without having to resume an old role.

A strong father or mentor can use authority to give another person agency. He can share what he learned at a cost and explain which part of it depends on the circumstances he was in. He can also admit when someone younger knows more about a place or an institution than he does. Such an exchange makes knowledge available in both directions. The adult keeps the responsibility that comes with his position and the younger person gets a way to contribute.

If you choose to tell a personal story in public, ask whom the story includes and what choice they have about it. An account can be honest and protect a family member's privacy. You can explain the part of your upbringing that shaped a value without describing a private crisis in detail. If telling the full story would ask somebody else to bear a cost, see whether the lesson can live with fewer details or a longer wait.

### Pass On What Can Survive a Change in Roles

A child can learn what a promise is from somebody who keeps one when it is inconvenient. A younger worker can learn how to take correction from a supervisor who can receive it in return. A relative who hears a difficult truth and remains present can give a young person reason to speak honestly in another relationship. These lessons matter even when the person teaching them has no official role or title.

You can decide which part of a role you want to carry into another setting. A coach may be good at building regular practice, and a child at home may need that patience more than another performance review. A manager may be skilled at anticipating problems, and a friend may need them to hear the problem before suggesting a fix. Skills are useful when the relationship gives them the right job. Ask what the person wants before assuming your familiar contribution is the one they need.

If someone placed trust in you before you gained a title, you may be tempted to think they were simply recognizing your future standing. They may have been looking for things you still have to practice: showing up, respecting a confidence, or making repair when you get something wrong. Keep those actions available in what you do now. If a new role makes it harder to spend time as you used to, explain what you can offer and honor the smaller promise.

You can change how you understand your past and still give somebody else a stable example today. Speak accurately about what you know, admit what you mean to learn and decide whose perspective deserves to be heard. The account of yourself you can live with becomes part of what other people inherit.

A word you use to describe yourself can become more useful once it refers to something you do. If you call yourself a mentor, make an appointment you can keep. If you describe yourself as somebody's son, daughter or child, think about which part of the relationship you want to carry forward. You can live out a useful lesson even when the relationship itself is complicated. The point is to choose a practice you can recognize as your own.

## The Final Word

A title may introduce you. The way you use it gives people more to go on.

## Closing questions

What could you change about how you present yourself and still feel true to your values?

How would the people affected by your decisions describe what your title gives you the power to do?

Which part of your story do you want to claim, and which part do you prefer to keep private?
