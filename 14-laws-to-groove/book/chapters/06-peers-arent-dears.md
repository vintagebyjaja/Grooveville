# LAW 06 — PEERS AREN'T DEARS

## The Law

Familiarity does not excuse disrespect.

## The Story

Fictional illustration.

At the restaurant everybody laughed before they saw Kendra's face. Malik had made a joke about why she was suddenly careful about money. He had used something she told him during the week she left her old apartment. By the time the check came, another person at the table had asked her how much she owed.

Kendra paid for her meal and left. In the group chat, Malik sent a photograph of the dessert menu with a message about the people who couldn't take a joke. She asked him to delete the message and call her the next day.

On the call she told him what she had said to him in confidence. He remembered the conversation. She asked who else had heard. He said just the people at the table, and then corrected himself. He had told another friend earlier in the week.

Malik wanted to explain that people in their group told jokes about one another all the time. Kendra reminded him that when his sister was ill, she had asked before giving even their closest friends an update. He remembered how relieved he'd been. They had already agreed, through what they did for one another, on a standard he could recognize.

He offered to tell the group he had repeated private information. Kendra asked him to say that much and to leave the details out. She also told him she would be keeping some distance. He sent the correction and gave her the distance. Several months passed before she asked whether he wanted to get lunch.

## The Reality

The same words can land differently in a room with an audience. A close friend may know how to embarrass you with a detail a stranger would miss. A group can make it difficult to say stop by demanding that the person who was hurt keep everybody else comfortable. Some people laugh because they are choosing how to get through dinner. Check with them later if you have reason to wonder.

Public teasing and private confidence call for different kinds of repair. A correction in front of other people can withdraw an insult. When the harm was sharing a secret, repeating the secret inside the apology can spread it further. Let the person affected have a say in who receives the correction and what it contains.

## The Lesson

Friendship gives us chances to learn what another person finds funny. It gives us responsibilities when we learn that we went too far. If you made the joke, you can put your defense aside long enough to hear its effects. You can also tell the people who heard it that it was out of line. A short correction becomes more credible when the group sees you stop doing the thing you corrected.

The same standards apply when you are receiving the joke. If a friend says a comment about their marriage, work or body is off limits, the fact that you accepted a similar comment last week tells you little about what they should accept. You can protect other people's dignity at a table even while you are learning how to protect your own.

Peer pressure can be quieter than a dare. It can sound like “we've always done it this way,” spoken by a person who knows what it will cost you to object. A boundary becomes practical when you decide what you will do if the behavior continues. That may mean leaving early, giving the group less personal information, or spending time with individual friends in a different setting.

## The Groove

Name the behavior in a sentence the other person can recognize. Ask for one change you can observe. If someone broke a confidence, ask who heard it and decide what correction would help you. If you broke it, provide the full account to the person affected and follow their lead on repair. A person may need time before they trust you with anything more.

## The Final Word

When Kendra met Malik for lunch, they talked about the food, the weather and a building being torn down near her work. She had chosen how much of her life she wanted to bring to the table. He let that be enough.
