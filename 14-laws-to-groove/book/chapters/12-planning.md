# LAW 12 — PLANNING

## Opening questions

What are you getting ready for at the moment?

Which part of a plan do you usually leave until the last minute?

When plans change, who do you tend to call?

## Understanding the Law

**Proper Pre-Planning Prevents Piss Poor Performance. Piss Poor Performance Promotes Pain.**

The 12 Ps describe a chain of consequences. Preparation helps you identify dependencies before a decision becomes expensive. Execution turns the plan into action. Responsibility includes seeing whose time or money is exposed when something goes wrong.

## The Reality Behind It

A plan describes how a result will happen. An estimate describes what resources it may take. A contingency identifies what to do if an assumption fails. The U.S. Government Accountability Office's 2015 Schedule Assessment Guide explains why dependencies, schedule risk and regular updates matter in complex projects. The same questions can help a household move, though a household can answer them with a much simpler document.

### Estimate From What Happened Last Time

In a 1994 study series, Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross explored why people underestimate how long tasks will take. In their experiments and studies, people's forecasts often focused on the plan ahead even when past tasks offered a reason to expect delay. Considering relevant past experience could help under some conditions. Their work investigated predictions, and the settings do not justify treating every missed deadline as a psychological error. Sometimes the underlying rules, resources or dependencies actually changed.

Start by comparing the new task with something that happened. If last year's event took four hours to set up, write down what those hours included. Decide which parts are genuinely simpler this year. If the new venue has a different loading entrance and no elevator, an optimistic estimate needs a reason. Ask the people doing the work where most time went. A single labor estimate from someone who stayed until the last table was folded may be more informative than a plan written at the beginning of the day.

Plans often become too optimistic when they assume nobody will have another responsibility at the same time. An approval may take minutes to sign and days to reach the approver. A truck can make the trip in half an hour once it has been loaded. A check-in may be short after everyone has found a way to be there. Keep elapsed time distinct from working time and leave room for waiting that has its own cause.

If a deadline is firm, deal with the difficulty early. Prepare a smaller version that reaches the essential goal. Ask what money or help would make the original plan feasible. If you can change the date, do that while other people still have options. If all three are unavailable, tell the people who will have to absorb the risk. They should be able to help decide how their time or money is used.

## Where We Go Wrong

A date on a calendar can give the appearance of preparation. Consider a family moving on Saturday whose keys will be released after the old apartment must be empty. Scheduling a truck solves one dependency. Confirming when the keys change hands solves another. If nobody checks, a missed handoff can cost the family another day of rental fees and someone else's time off work.

### Plan Around the Handoff

If a group plans a Saturday event, it can mark opening time in large letters and still fail to agree on who gets the key. Find the tasks that depend on another task ending first. The driver needs an address before collecting equipment. The host needs the room set before trying sound. The accessible route should be checked before invitations tell people which entrance to use. Give each handoff a name and a time.

A useful contingency names its trigger. “Ask someone else to pick it up if necessary” leaves everyone to decide what necessary means. “If the van has not been confirmed by Thursday at noon, arrange pickup with the backup driver” can be acted on before the regular driver disappears. Check that the backup has actually agreed and that the cost has an owner. A plan can assign authority in the same paragraph that assigns work.

The Government Accountability Office's scheduling guidance addresses large and complicated programs. A household can use its concepts without reproducing its documentation. A handwritten plan with a few confirmed dependencies is enough for some moves. A larger event with permits, paid contractors and people traveling from out of town may need written milestones and a way to notify everyone when they change. Match the documentation to the consequences of error.

Afterward, identify which part went differently and what the group will do next time. If you write only that the project was a success, people may never hear that cleanup took three unpaid hours from the same two people. A useful review tells you which expectation needs revising and who should be invited to help revise it.

## Applying the Law

Write the result in one sentence and work backward from the deadline. Assign every handoff to a person who has accepted it. Mark the first date you can verify each assumption. Discuss which cost rises fastest if work slips and decide who can authorize the backup. On the day of execution, keep the plan available where the people doing the work can see it. Update it when facts change.

### Use the Plan While You Are Doing the Work

A plan is most valuable when people can change it publicly enough to keep working together. Give the people doing the work one place to check current decisions. An invitation, a schedule and a message to volunteers can each carry useful information. If the event moves and only one of those places changes, people may continue to act on different versions. Decide who can update the plan, who has to hear about the change and what happens if someone is away.

A test run can expose a dependency that no one would have written down. Walk the route a visitor will take. Try opening the room with the key you have. Check whether the person expected to register guests can read the list they will receive. If there is no time for a full rehearsal, test the thing that would cost the most to discover too late. A check that produces a specific answer has more value than an instruction to be prepared.

The planning habit can become expensive if it discourages people from acting until every uncertainty disappears. List which matters can be resolved while work continues and which need a decision first. A family can price movers while waiting to hear when a new address is available. It may choose to wait before paying a deposit whose return depends on dates. A community project may authorize materials in stages as volunteers, permits and funding become available. A plan is a way of making those choices explicit.

Budget for the work that continues after the main event. Receipts have to be collected, borrowed equipment returned and a room cleaned. Find out which obligations begin only when the visible work is done. The people who inherit those jobs should be able to review the plan before the rest of the group announces success.

## The Community Connection

Before announcing a community event, check accessibility, weather, permission, transport, money and cleanup. Find out who has keys and who can answer after hours. Planning gets more accurate when the people asked to perform the work can review it while changes are still affordable.

### Put the Costs Where People Can See Them

A family plan can move expenses around without reducing them. Moving on a weekday might cut the truck charge and cost a relative a day of pay. Buying materials early might save money and expose somebody to a cost they cannot easily recover. Compare what each arrangement requires and who has the resources to cover it. Ask whether people offered the help you have assumed and whether they were told how long the work would take.

Small groups need plans they can carry. A document nobody reads can hide uncertainty behind its length. Keep a short version that answers the questions people ask most often. Who is responsible? Which date matters? What has to happen first? Who can authorize a change? If the work exposes people to significant safety or financial risk, put the relevant instructions in front of them before the work begins. Ask somebody who was not in the planning meeting to explain what they would do in a common case.

A backup can carry its own risks. Going ahead in a different room might solve a weather problem and make the event inaccessible to some people. A substitute driver may be available but lack a vehicle large enough for the equipment. A member of the group may agree to cover an important job as a courtesy, then discover it extends beyond the time they can give. Check whether the alternative preserves the purpose that made the plan important in the first place.

Review what happened with the people closest to the work. Ask which problems were foreseeable and which could only be discovered in use. Someone may have warned you of a problem and received no answer. Someone else may have improvised a good solution that can become part of the next plan. Record the change at the place where future organizers will actually look for instructions. This is how preparation becomes an inheritance instead of a story about the people who worked hardest.

### Plan for the People Who Will Use the Result

A project can produce what the plan described and still fall short of what people needed. A neighborhood group might arrange a workshop at the cheapest available venue and discover that families cannot get there after work. A school might distribute instructions and learn that the students who need them cannot read them in the form provided. Decide how you will find out whether the plan works for the user while there is still time to change it. Ask for feedback at the point where a person has to use something, not only at the end of a meeting about it.

Plan for steps that somebody else has authority to delay. A person may need a permit before a room can be used, or approval before a payment can be made. Ask which information the approving person needs and when they can consider it. Track the date you sent the request and what they said about the next step. If a deadline depends on an approval, avoid describing it as fully confirmed until you can responsibly make that promise. A conditional schedule can give people time to prepare while acknowledging where the decision stands.

Complicated projects often need more than one estimate. Define ordinary cost, a plausible higher cost and the point at which someone will decide whether to continue. The estimates should include expenses somebody else would absorb, such as time off, travel or supervision. If you ask people to make another contribution after the estimate changes, explain which assumption failed. Somebody who agreed to the first amount should have a chance to decide about the second.

Practice delegating with enough information to act. A person can be willing to manage a task and still need a budget, contact information and authority to make a small change. Ask them to describe what they will do first and where they would check if something changes. That is a chance to identify a missing handoff before the person is left alone with the responsibility. Check whether anyone else will be giving them different instructions.

At the end of a project, write one change you can use again. Put a reminder on the first page of the plan rather than leaving the recommendation at the end of a long report. If the problem was a missing decision, move it earlier. If the problem was uncertain authority, identify who can act. The 12 Ps matter when an observation from one effort reduces the work, delay or pain the next group will have to absorb.

Before you call a plan ready, let one person explain how the day starts and another explain how it ends. The first may find that nobody has agreed to open the room. The second may find that the last driver has to leave before equipment is packed. Both can make useful corrections before the work begins. A plan that accounts for those ordinary details is often the one people can actually carry out together.

## The Final Word

The person who inherits a problem should have a chance to help prevent it.

## Closing questions

What would tell you it is time to use your backup plan?

How could you make one upcoming handoff easier for both people?
