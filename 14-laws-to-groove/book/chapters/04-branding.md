# LAW 04 — BRANDING

## Opening questions

How do you usually describe yourself to somebody you just met?

What do you hope people notice about the way you work?

When do you feel most comfortable speaking for yourself?

## Understanding the Law

Branding concerns the relationship between character, reputation and power. Character describes what you practice. Reputation describes what other people have heard or observed. Power determines whose description reaches the person making a decision. You can make your work easier to recognize while asking institutions to judge it fairly.

Know your worth and expect respect. Think of internal net worth as the value you recognize in yourself, without putting a price on it. That recognition can shape how your confidence comes across in public. It can help you speak clearly about what you contribute, acknowledge what you are still learning and set a boundary without asking the room to vote on your dignity. Confidence can be quiet. People deserve respect on the days they cannot project it, too.

### Notice Who Gets to Define Professionalism

A workplace may have good reasons to require certain clothing, language or presentation. Protective equipment has a job. A clear explanation matters when someone has to act on instructions. A quick assessment of whether someone “looks the part” can be harder to defend. Ask what responsibility the standard relates to, whether people receive a chance to meet it and whether the same conduct receives the same treatment. If the answer changes according to who is in the room, the standard itself deserves examination.

People may adapt because they want a goal more than they want to fight about every expectation. A worker can learn a formal way to present a proposal and use it confidently. Fairness still depends on how institutions apply their criteria. Credentials, language and presentation do not determine whether someone deserves respect. We can understand the choices people make under pressure without suggesting their choices caused someone else to discriminate.

Consider a school or work group inviting nominations for a leadership opportunity. A person who holds the floor easily may come to mind first. Another person may make sure that tools are available, send instructions everyone can follow and prevent assignments from falling through. If the process defines leadership by results, both kinds of work can be considered. If the process depends on who appears decisive in a meeting, it might reward only the contribution the group recognizes quickly. Asking reviewers to specify the evidence can broaden the field without asking them to stop exercising judgment.

## The Reality Behind It

Black workers may have to confront labels placed on patterns of speech, appearance or behavior. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission's 2006 race and color guidance discusses employment decisions linked to racial characteristics and stereotypes. It describes the agency's interpretation and enforcement framework. A particular dispute depends on its facts and applicable law.

### Recover From a Reputation You Didn't Choose

A person learning that a misleading story has traveled can be tempted to correct it with everybody who might have heard it. Identify who depends on the account for a decision. If a prospective supervisor has been told you left a project unfinished, bring the actual handoff record and explain which part you completed. If an acquaintance heard something personal, consider whether responding will give the story more reach. The scale of the correction can follow the scale of the risk.

An institution should take care when it passes along judgments no one can verify. If a manager says an employee is “hard to work with,” the claim should prompt questions about the work and whose perspective is represented. If there has been a real breakdown, a description of what occurred can help the next person make a better assignment or offer useful support. A label alone gives them little power to act fairly. It may simply give the first manager a second chance to decide somebody's future.

Consider the role of social media. A public account can show work, connect a person with customers or make it easier to find people with similar interests. It can also put a past statement into a present context. If you post in a professional role, learn which obligations come with speaking for others. When you speak personally, decide which parts of your private life you want an audience to interpret. The audience may include people you did not intend to reach.

## Where We Go Wrong

Vague praise and vague criticism are both difficult to use. When someone calls you “leadership material,” they may be offering an opportunity or referring to a style they happen to recognize. When another person calls you “intense,” ask when that description affected the work. Treat the answer as evidence you can examine. Your right to fair treatment is already yours.

### Document the Work Without Performing It for the Room

A person trying to make a reputation can spend most of a day making visible work look larger. Some useful work has a quiet result. The equipment failure that never happened, the agreement that survived a difficult transition and the new worker who learned the process may all be easy to overlook. Record the problem, your responsibility and the outcome close to when the work happened. Carry information that is yours to share. Leave out confidential details and give other contributors credit.

A worker can learn to describe a contribution without becoming their own full-time publicist. An annual evaluation might ask for evidence that you improved a service. Bring an example and explain why you believe the change helped. If you do not know whether the improvement lasted, say that. There is credibility in saying where your evidence ends. A person newer to the work may notice a limit that you missed and help you improve the next example.

A reputation can also become a constraint. Someone known for resolving conflict can become the person everybody expects to absorb it. Someone known for working late can be overlooked when they ask for a more sustainable schedule. Ask what new behavior the group has reason to expect from you. You may need to name a boundary explicitly. The reputation can change as the group sees how you live with it.

If a description rests on a stereotype, the person described should not carry the whole burden of correction. A supervisor can require that judgments bear on the work. A colleague can ask whether similar conduct received a similar description. An institution can audit patterns across complaints and promotion recommendations. Specific records allow those questions to address more than one exchange.

## Applying the Law

Choose one quality you want people to associate with your work. Identify a behavior that makes it visible. To build trust in your estimates, explain how you arrived at the range and tell colleagues when the underlying facts change. When you receive a vague evaluation, ask for a specific example, the standard that applied and how improvement will be assessed. Preserve relevant records and seek qualified advice if discriminatory treatment is a concern.

### Your Confidence Has More Than One Audience

Sometimes you address the outward audience while you are still deciding what you believe about the work. A new employee may need to say they can complete a task and also ask where the instructions are. An experienced person may need to tell a customer what they can deliver and decline an additional request. Confidence can be a steady account of your capacity. That account is more useful than a performance that makes everybody believe you never have to ask a question.

Listen for the difference between criticism of a decision and a verdict about your value. If someone identifies a mistake, ask what needs repair and how to avoid it next time. If they offer a degrading label, ask whether they can point to a relevant behavior. You can choose what information to use. Recognizing your worth can also help you hear correction without treating it as evidence that everything you have done is in doubt.

People with influence can spend some of it on a fairer account of another person's work. If you repeat an idea in a meeting, name who brought it to you. If a written evaluation uses a loaded phrase, ask what happened and whether the writer would describe the same behavior in another employee the same way. If you have authority to make the criteria public, use it. Visibility belongs to the work and the people doing it.

## The Community Connection

If you supervise, compare evaluations for people who performed similar work. Ask how each adjective was earned and whether you applied the same standard. A coworker can help by crediting the person responsible for an idea in the same room where that credit has value.

### Let Your Public Account Have Some Boundaries

A business can make its reputation easier to check through a clear price, written scope and a dependable way to resolve complaints. Customers can judge the service as delivered. One bad review may give an owner a useful detail or may present a claim the owner can correct with records. Respond to the issue and protect information the customer did not intend to make public. A visible answer can serve people who encounter the disagreement long after both parties have moved on.

When you teach somebody to introduce themselves, ask which part of their contribution they are proud to explain. A first-generation applicant may think everyone else has an impressive account to offer. Their experience helping relatives navigate a service, resolving a conflict or maintaining equipment may give them evidence of skills an evaluator has asked to see. Help them connect the skill to the task. Leave them control over which parts of their family or identity they use to do that.

Allow a difference between visibility and dignity. A person who passes a small shop every day may never know who keeps the books. The person doing that work still deserves to be paid, heard and given credit in the setting where it matters. A healthy approach to reputation lets good work become recognizable without asking everybody to spend their day making themselves seen.

### Decide What Kind of Evidence You Can Offer

Ask someone who knows your work to describe one ordinary example of how you do it. You may learn that they rely on you to make instructions clear or notice a missing step early. Offer a detail from the same example that shows how you learned the skill. If your contribution was shared, say who helped and which part you handled. When a review demands a short account, these details give you something accurate to use.

A negative description can sometimes tell you how to give other people more room. If a colleague says you make it hard to ask questions during a demonstration, ask where they would have paused. They may be identifying a teachable part of your practice. You can add that pause without taking on a broad judgment about your character. If the criticism is too vague to test, ask the person making decisions what they need to observe.

You may decide to keep some strengths away from a public audience. Being willing to help someone handle a private difficulty is a form of reliability. Receiving praise for it could require telling a story whose details are theirs. Recognize the work for yourself and exercise judgment about what you report. A reputation can be partial and truthful. The people you help can decide whether any of their account becomes part of it.

Other people can support your confidence without having to speak for you. They can open an opportunity, tell you what they have observed and leave room for you to describe your work. If you are in a position to do that for someone else, let them be more than the example you happened to notice. Give them a chance to show you what you would have missed.

## The Final Word

Give people evidence of your character. Ask them to take responsibility for the way they describe it.

## Closing questions

What can you value about yourself even when the room fails to see it?

Which label would you ask someone to back up with an example?

Whose work could you give clearer credit the next time you are in a position to do so?
