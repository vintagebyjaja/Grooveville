# LAW 01 — REFLECTION

## Understanding the Law

Reflection means reviewing the distance between what happened and what you decided it meant. The central claim of this law is that you can improve a judgment by making its basis visible. Begin with a decision small enough to recount precisely. What did the person say? What did you assume? How did the assumption shape the next thing you did?

This exercise includes your strengths. If you handled a crisis well, identify which preparation, practical knowledge and help from other people made it possible. You gain something you can repeat. You also have a chance to give credit.

## The Reality Behind It

Our interpretations use information we gathered before entering the room. A family instruction such as “keep a copy of everything” may come from a specific experience of being dismissed. Ask what the rule protected and whether that protection is still needed. A warning can contain sound judgment even when it was applied too broadly.

Institutions also need to compare their judgments with observable outcomes. Bertrand and Mullainathan's field experiment sent fictitious applications to job advertisements in Boston and Chicago. Applications with names associated with White applicants drew more callbacks than comparable applications with names associated with Black applicants. The researchers observed callbacks in a defined labor market. Their method could reveal unequal treatment at that stage while leaving the individual employers' reasoning unobserved. [1]

## Where We Go Wrong

An explanation becomes hard to revise when it protects something we value about ourselves. A supervisor who prides herself on moving quickly may describe confidence as evidence. An employee who has been singled out before may blame herself for failing to find precisely the right words to prevent it. The first needs to ask what supports her judgment. The second deserves an honest account of who had decision-making power.

## Applying the Law

Use two columns. Put observable details on the left and your interpretations on the right. Underline the interpretation you acted on. Write a plausible alternative and identify one question or record that could help you choose between them. When an immediate decision is needed, act on the best information you have and say what remains open.

If a mistake affected a person publicly, consider where the correction has to travel. A private apology can repair part of a relationship. Correcting the evaluation, incident report or meeting record addresses a separate harm. Ask the affected person how they want private details handled.

## The Community Connection

Try this method when reviewing who gets nominated, disciplined or promoted. Examine what decision makers documented and compare how they treated similar cases. As a child or new employee you may have had little access to the record. If you now supervise, teach or lead, you can give others a chance to see and respond to what is being said about them.

## The Final Word

Your first account may explain why you acted. A fuller account can tell you what to do next.

## Food for Thought

Which judgment about yourself would you revise if you included the help or obstacles you faced?

Who has a chance to correct the record when your school, workplace or community group makes a mistake?

What can an audit experiment establish about discrimination, and what additional evidence should guide a remedy?
