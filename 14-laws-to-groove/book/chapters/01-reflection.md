# LAW 01 — REFLECTION

## The Law

Learn to recognize the part of a situation you brought with you. You will have a better chance of seeing what everybody else brought, too.

## The Story

Fictional illustration.

The first bus was due out at five twenty. At four forty-eight it sat above the shallow inspection pit with a yellow tag on the driver's seat and the hood propped open. Nia knew the tag by its corner. Jalen always folded the corner down before he wrote on it, as if making a place for his finger to land.

“Who put that bus back on the board?” she asked.

The board listed the vehicles dispatch expected to use that morning. A name beside a number meant somebody had said the vehicle could go. Nia had taken this bus off the list before leaving the previous afternoon. A driver had reported an intermittent warning light. The warning hadn't appeared for Nia. She had left a note for the morning crew to check the bus again before releasing it.

“I wrote the tag,” Jalen said from across the bay.

“Then you know why we're standing here.”

There were three other people within earshot. One of them looked up at the board. Jalen took a breath, as if he meant to answer, and went back to the bench.

Nia called dispatch and held the bus. A spare took the route a few minutes late. By breakfast, the shop supervisor had asked Nia to enter an incident note. She wrote that the apprentice had put a bus back in service before an open warning had been cleared. The sentence took less than a minute. It was going to become part of Jalen's record for months.

A driver named Evelyn came through the bay after the rush. She had spent long enough around the shop to know which conversations waited until the noise died down.

“Did he tell you he cleared it?”

“He said he wrote the tag.”

“What did the tag say?”

Nia remembered the corner. She remembered that it was yellow. She had looked at it through the glass and left it on the seat.

They went out together. The tag showed the time Jalen had arrived and the words **Awaiting repeat check. Hold for lead.** Evelyn read it without saying anything. A carbon copy in the dispatch sleeve showed a different bus number on the first run sheet. Someone had corrected the number in the wrong column when copying the sheet onto the board. The shop supervisor tracked down the mismatch that morning.

Jalen had come in early to get familiar with the warning system. He had written exactly what Nia would have wanted him to write. When he saw his bus on the board, he was as surprised as she was. He had expected to be asked what he found. When Nia said her piece across the bay, he took her tone to mean the question had already been settled.

On the other end of the shop, Nia sat with the incident note open. Her first impulse was to call Evelyn back and explain that she had only been trying to keep the bus off the road. This was true. She had made a sound decision about the bus. It was also the part of the story that cost her the least to tell.

Early in her career, Nia had reported a problem on a vehicle that another technician had signed off. The afternoon supervisor had asked, in front of the crew, whether she ever intended to trust anybody who knew more than she did. Later, when the same problem came up, that supervisor asked why she hadn't documented it more fully. She kept a notebook after that. The notebook helped. So did her decision to learn how dispatch and the shop passed information back and forth. She took pride in being hard to surprise.

There was a detail about that old exchange she seldom remembered. When the afternoon supervisor challenged her, a technician named Ruth had waited by the time clock and asked what Nia had seen on the vehicle. Ruth's question had given her a way to describe the problem in plain language and write a better record. Nia usually remembered the moment as herself alone against the entire room. That was how it had felt through most of the afternoon. Ruth had been there, too.

Nia found Jalen at the end of the shift. He was wrapping a sandwich in the paper it had come in. She asked whether he had a minute.

“I heard you say you wrote the tag and I filled in the rest. I had the right call on holding the bus. I got your part wrong.”

He folded the wrapper twice.

“I could have spoken up,” he said. “I thought it might sound like I was giving you an excuse.”

“You should be able to finish a sentence when we're talking about your work,” Nia said. “I sent a note with the wrong account in it. I'm getting that corrected.”

Jalen asked if the apprentice coordinator had already seen it. That question told Nia something she had missed. She had been thinking about the morning's embarrassment. He had been thinking about the next time somebody decided whether he was ready to take on a bigger job.

The supervisor amended the record with the dispatch findings and shared the correction with everyone who had received the first note. At the next shift handover, Nia said she had identified the wrong person and that Jalen's hold tag had been correct. The explanation took a few sentences. Several people glanced at the board. Jalen asked to see both versions of the incident note. Nia gave him the names of the people who could get him a copy, then stayed while he asked.

Two weeks later they had another discrepancy. This time Nia asked Jalen to walk her through the sequence, including the parts he thought she knew. There was a lot he thought she knew. She had been out the day dispatch changed its morning worksheet. Neither of them had thought to mention it before.

## The Reality

Most people leave a disagreement with a quicker memory of the other person's behavior than of their own. You remember being interrupted. Ask the person across from you and they may remember waiting to hear the end of a question that never came. A reasonable account can include both experiences. It still matters who had the opportunity to check their assumptions before acting. Nia had enough authority to put her version in a record. Jalen had to ask who had seen it.

Workplaces depend on quick judgments. Someone has to decide whether a vehicle stays put. A nurse has to decide which call needs attention first. A teacher may need to step between children before there is time to hear either account. Quick action and final judgment can operate on different clocks. Nia had enough evidence to hold the bus. She needed more evidence to say who had put it back on the board. A shop that makes room for that distinction can protect people on the route and people on the crew at the same time.

Race influences some of the judgments people make even when the decision appears to involve ordinary work and ordinary paperwork. In an experiment published in 2004, economists Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan sent fictitious résumés in response to help-wanted advertisements in Boston and Chicago. They assigned names associated with being Black or white to the applications and measured employer callbacks. The otherwise comparable applications with names associated with being white received more callbacks. The researchers could observe the invitations to interview. They could not hear the deliberations of the people reading the applications. Their design let them test whether changing the name attached to a résumé changed what happened next.

Put yourself in the position of an applicant looking at a quiet inbox. You might see each missing response as a sign that you needed a better qualification, another year in your field or a different way to describe what you had already done. Any of those might help in a particular search. The experiment shows why an individual applicant's experience, even when carefully examined, supplies only part of the explanation. It also shows why the employer's description of itself supplies only part. A workplace may believe it welcomes everybody. A review of who advances, according to which criteria and whose evaluations, can give it something specific to examine.

The same distinction matters in how people take in advice from their families. A Black parent who tells a child to keep copies of every school email may have a precise memory of which conversation changed when a teacher's words were available in writing. Another parent may warn that insisting on a written answer draws unwanted attention. Advice can differ within one family because the adults faced different risks or learned in different places. If you grew up with a warning that still follows you, ask what happened to the person who first gave it. Sometimes they will tell you. Sometimes you will have enough context to understand how they came by it. That knowledge gives you a chance to decide how to use the warning today.

Nia's notebook belongs in this part of the story. It served a real purpose. Putting things on paper made it harder for someone else to decide later what she had said. The notebook also gave her a practice she could continue when she had more authority. In the incident report, she took the familiar form of writing something down and skipped the work that gave the form value. She needed to distinguish between what she had seen and what she had concluded. An institution can do the same by making the difference visible in its forms, its meetings and its records. Leaving a line for the source of an allegation can do more than sending people to a meeting about communication.

## The Lesson

When people are asked to reflect, they often produce a character sketch. I work hard. I give too much. I care what people think. The sentences may be accurate. They cover so much ground that it is difficult to do anything with them. A smaller unit of observation can help. Think of the last time a person surprised you in a way that made you angry. What did you hear first? What did you decide that sentence meant? What was the next thing you did? Would somebody watching from the doorway have known what you thought you were responding to?

Nia heard, “I wrote the tag.” Her own experience supplied a completed story: he handled the bus, he cleared it, and he was waiting to see whether somebody senior would make him answer for it. She knew the sort of morning that story produced. Her body was ready to get ahead of it. Under pressure, a familiar explanation can feel finished before you have decided to believe it. Asking one more question is sometimes enough to discover how much of the explanation came from you.

A strength is worth examining alongside a fault. Nia noticed a genuine risk. She knew enough about operations to get dispatch to send out a spare. When people tell her to be patient, she may hear an invitation to ignore the urgency of a real problem. The repair asks something more specific. She can keep the decisiveness and use it for the next action justified by the evidence. Take the vehicle off the list. Read the tag. Ask the person who wrote it to explain what happened. There is an order to these steps, and the order protects her judgment from having to accomplish everything at once.

It also helps to look for the action your story makes convenient. If you have decided that your cousin never calls because she thinks she's better than the rest of the family, you can spend a long time discussing her attitude without calling her. If you notice that the last time she told you something private, it came up at dinner, you have a different task. Some of us will do quite a bit of thinking before we do the one thing our thinking has made obvious. A private diagnosis of your own habits is easier to control than a conversation with a person who gets to tell you why they stopped trusting you.

Repair follows the reach of the mistake. If the accusation traveled across the shop, a correction belongs where the accusation traveled. If it entered a record, it needs to be corrected in that record. There is a particular frustration in receiving a heartfelt apology while the person with authority over your schedule still believes the first account. Nia had a feeling to express and a record to amend. Jalen had reason to care about both and to decide when the repair was enough.

Reflection also means giving yourself a fair account. If you have been repeatedly dismissed, you may hear critical advice as one more demand to prove that you can handle mistreatment gracefully. You can examine a specific choice while keeping an accurate account of what was done to you. If a manager changes your hours after you raise a concern, write down the dates and preserve the actual messages. Ask someone knowledgeable what options are available. You can think about the conversation and pursue the issue at the same time. The manager's authority gives their actions a weight that your private regret about a phrase cannot carry.

A friend, colleague or family member can be a steady witness if they have enough room to disagree with you. The question “What did the tag say?” was small enough for Nia to hear. It came from a person who understood the work and had listened to the complaint. Sometimes we ask people to take our side at the very moment when we most need them to help us find out what happened. Tell them which service you're asking for. If you need someone to sit with you before you can hear any questions, say so. If you're asking for their judgment, give them the details that could change it.

One reason this takes time is that a memory often preserves the answer to the question you were living with. Nia remembered, “Who stood up for me when I needed it?” In the room, almost nobody had. At the time clock, Ruth did something quieter. Both moments mattered. Remembering Ruth gives Nia a model she can use the next time a young technician has something to say. Paying attention to the whole day returns something to her that a true but narrow memory had left behind.

An accurate account may also reveal a success you had been treating as an accident. Perhaps you knew what questions to ask because you listened well when your neighbor explained how her business worked. Perhaps you stayed calm because you had practiced a difficult conversation with someone who could interrupt you without making you feel foolish. When you identify what helped, you can use it again. You can give the other person credit. A person who knows how they learned something is often in a better position to teach it.

## The Groove

**Give an episode its own page.** Choose a recent exchange you keep replaying. At the top of the page, give it a date and a place. In the first paragraph, write what a camera or a voice recorder could have captured. In the next, write what you took those details to mean. Include the part that makes you look petty, frightened or overly certain. A useful page can hold an ordinary misunderstanding without turning it into a verdict on the rest of your life.

**Follow the inference.** Underline the sentence where you moved from something you observed to an explanation for it. Write down one other explanation consistent with the facts. A colleague who stopped sharing work with you might be offended. They might believe the assignment changed. Ask a question that gives them room to supply information: “I noticed I haven't been getting the Tuesday drafts. Can you tell me how the handoff works now?” If they offer a specific answer, let it change the story where it can.

**Ask what an old rule was protecting.** Write down an instruction you still hear in a parent's, neighbor's or coach's voice. Try to recall a time when the rule worked. Then recall a time you kept applying it after circumstances changed. If you were taught never to ask for help until you had tried everything, the advice may have protected you from depending on an unreliable person. It might also keep you from using the support available on a team that expects you to ask early. Try adjusting the rule in one setting where you can observe how people respond.

**Match the repair to the consequence.** Name what you did, identify who had to live with it, and work out what you can change. If you gave the wrong account of a coworker in a meeting, ask the chair to correct the minutes and send the corrected account to the attendees. If you shared a friend's news without permission, tell them who heard it and stop passing it along. Your friend may want a different remedy from the one you would choose. Part of returning their dignity is hearing what they ask for.

**Write a question near the place the problem occurs.** A prompt in a notebook helps if the notebook is there when a choice has to be made. A supervisor could put “Who confirmed the handoff?” beside the box where a name gets entered on a form. At home, a parent who often starts arguments while everybody is trying to get out the door might set an afternoon reminder to revisit a disputed plan. Put the interruption close enough to your usual habit that you have a chance to catch yourself.

**Review one good call.** Choose something you handled well this month. List the preparation, help, timing and judgment that made it possible. Pay particular attention to someone whose contribution would be invisible in a short version of the story. Tell them what you noticed. Keep the letter or the note if it helps you remember what you can do again.

## The Final Word

By the time Nia took her coat off that morning, she already had a reason to distrust what's written on a board. Experience had earned her that much. Jalen gave her a reason to look at the tag before deciding whose name belonged in the report. She could have carried both pieces of knowledge into the bay from the beginning, if she had made room for the second one.

A good account of your life leaves room for evidence you haven't heard yet. The next person whose sentence you let finish may tell you what you need to know about yourself.
