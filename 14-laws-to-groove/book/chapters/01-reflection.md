# LAW 01 — REFLECTION

## Opening questions

When something goes wrong, whose part in it do you usually think about first?

What do people who know you well say you do under pressure?

Which lesson from your upbringing do you still use without thinking much about it?

## Understanding the Law

Reflection means reviewing the distance between what happened and what you decided it meant. The central claim of this law is that you can improve a judgment by making its basis visible. Begin with a decision small enough to recount precisely. What did the person say? What did you assume? How did the assumption shape the next thing you did?

This exercise includes your strengths. If you handled a crisis well, identify which preparation, practical knowledge and help from other people made it possible. You gain something you can repeat. You also have a chance to give credit.

### Examine the Habit Before You Explain It

Identity enters a room with us. You may think of yourself as the one who can take charge when a situation gets messy, the one who stays out of other people's business or the one who notices when somebody is being overlooked. These descriptions can tell the truth while leaving out the occasions when they have become too broad. The first person may assume a responsibility belongs to them when a colleague is already handling it. The second may stay quiet when their support would carry weight. The third may tell someone else's story in a way that costs them privacy. Reflection asks how a quality operated in a particular situation. It gives you a way to keep the quality without granting it authority over every decision.

Try tracing a pattern across three settings. Think about when you react strongly to being corrected at work, in your family or with friends. Record who was present, how the correction was made, and what happened if you challenged it. You may find a difference between your response to public and private correction. You may find that one setting already gives you room to explain yourself. That difference suggests a request you can make. It can also show you what kind of room you should give people when you are in the position to correct them.

There is an important limit to the exercise. Repeated discrimination cannot be explained away by the quality of a person's reflection. If someone has dismissed your concern three times, looking for an alternative explanation can help you choose a next step. It can also become another way to move the burden onto yourself. When documentation would help, preserve it. When you can ask a trusted person with experience in the setting to look at the record, make that request. Decide which question is yours to answer and which question belongs to the person or institution making the decision.

## The Reality Behind It

Our interpretations use information we gathered before entering the room. A family instruction such as “keep a copy of everything” may come from a specific experience of being dismissed. Ask what the rule protected and whether that protection is still needed. A warning can contain sound judgment even when it was applied too broadly.

Institutions also need to compare their judgments with observable outcomes. In 2004, Bertrand and Mullainathan's field experiment sent fictitious applications to job advertisements in Boston and Chicago. Applications with names associated with White applicants drew more callbacks than comparable applications with names associated with Black applicants. The researchers observed callbacks in a defined labor market. Their method could reveal unequal treatment at that stage while leaving the individual employers' reasoning unobserved.

## Where We Go Wrong

An explanation becomes hard to revise when it protects something we value about ourselves. A supervisor who prides herself on moving quickly may describe confidence as evidence. An employee who has been singled out before may blame herself for failing to find precisely the right words to prevent it. The first needs to ask what supports her judgment. The second deserves an honest account of who had decision-making power.

### Review Decisions at the Level Where They Were Made

Start with one decision and the evidence it required. If you heard that a friend was upset, a call could settle whether the report was accurate. If you judged a coworker unreliable based on three late handoffs, check the three dates, who controlled the preceding step, and whether someone had changed the due time. A conclusion about an event needs less evidence than a label you intend to carry into every future encounter. The greater your authority over someone else, the more care you owe to that distinction.

Give yourself a chance to find evidence you did not expect. Suppose you spent a semester convinced that a teacher discouraged you because of a certain comment. The comment can still matter after you find that the teacher wrote a recommendation in your favor. The recommendation can matter even if the comment deserves a response. The exercise is to hold both pieces of information while you decide what to do. You can revise one part of an account without surrendering the part that remains well supported.

Looking back can also reveal what you did right without making success entirely your own. A promotion may have followed excellent work, a colleague mentioning your name and a manager leaving at the right time. If you recognize the contribution of all three, you know which effort to keep making and which conditions you cannot reproduce through effort alone. You may also notice a chance to recommend someone whose work you have seen.

Review can become avoidance when it turns every small decision into an all-night inquiry. Set the amount of review by the cost of getting it wrong. If you are choosing a restaurant, let a mistake be inexpensive. If you are setting someone's evaluation, looking into the record is part of the job. For decisions that cannot wait, act on what you can establish and decide when the assumptions should be reviewed.

## Applying the Law

Use two columns. Put observable details on the left and your interpretations on the right. Underline the interpretation you acted on. Write a plausible alternative and identify one question or record that could help you choose between them. When an immediate decision is needed, act on the best information you have and say what remains open.

If a mistake affected a person publicly, consider where the correction has to travel. A private apology can repair part of a relationship. Correcting the evaluation, incident report or meeting record addresses a separate harm. Ask the affected person how they want private details handled.

### Give Feedback a Form You Can Use

Ask a person who sees you in the situation you want to understand. “How am I doing?” can be a generous invitation with a difficult answer. “At the last two meetings, did people know which part of the task I was asking them to do?” gives someone an event and a point to examine. Tell them how you will use the answer. If the person depends on you for a reference, pay or a place in the group, consider how a direct criticism might cost them. Provide other ways to respond and accept that some people may choose to wait.

Receiving feedback is a decision of its own. You can ask for an example without turning the conversation into a defense. If somebody says you make decisions before hearing the people affected, ask which decision they mean and what information they wanted to share. You may find a misunderstanding you can correct or another way to consult people before the next decision is made. If the criticism is about a repeated behavior, repairing one event is a start. Look for the step in your routine that made it possible to repeat.

Reflection can help with praise as well. If a person thanks you for making a difficult setting easier, ask which part made the difference. Perhaps you gave them time to read before voting. Perhaps they appreciated an introduction you had forgotten making. Record what actually helped. You can repeat it or teach it to someone who will have a similar responsibility later.

## The Community Connection

Try this method when reviewing who gets nominated, disciplined or promoted. Examine what decision makers documented and compare how they treated similar cases. As a child or new employee you may have had little access to the record. If you now supervise, teach or lead, you can give others a chance to see and respond to what is being said about them.

### Who Gets to Correct the Record

An individual mistake can remain repairable if the person who made it still controls what happens next. Once an account enters an institution, other people may rely on it without seeing where it came from. A supervisor reads a previous evaluation. A teacher reads a referral before meeting a student. A new committee uses minutes written by people who have moved on. The record gives the first description a longer life than the conversation that produced it.

A procedure for correction can protect people who lack the power to persuade the original author. Imagine a youth organization that keeps incident notes. It could record dates, name the source of an observation and allow the person affected to submit a response. A correction should reach staff who rely on the original, with private details kept as limited as possible. If reviewing every correction would be burdensome, start with records that affect access, pay or discipline. Distinguish correcting a fact from recording a disagreement over its meaning.

There is a lesson here for anyone who keeps minutes or makes introductions. You are often the only witness to the choice of words. “Challenged the proposal” gives a future reader more room to decide what took place than “was combative.” If the conduct itself matters, describe it in enough detail that somebody else could reasonably disagree with you.

### Revisit a Judgment After It Has Had an Effect

An apology is a promise to take another person's experience seriously. It also gives you a chance to check what happened after the apology. If a parent corrected a child in front of relatives, the child might still worry that the relatives believe the first account. You can ask which part remains hard to put right. Avoid asking the child for reassurance that you are a good parent. Ask what the adults need to know now and who should tell them.

Set a time to see whether the change survived. If you agreed to ask before interrupting someone else's process, notice the next two occasions when you were tempted to go ahead. If an organization corrected an evaluation, ask whether the person deciding assignments works from the corrected record. Repair can fail quietly when the people first affected have moved on to another task. A check need not reopen every detail. It can simply confirm that the change reached the place you intended.

Give yourself permission to leave some things unresolved. People can disagree about how much one choice mattered. You can correct a detail, change what you do and still lack a full explanation for what happened. Reflection is useful because it gives you more than one chance to learn from the experience. You do not have to arrive at a verdict about your whole life to make a better decision tomorrow.

When you return to a decision later, write a short account before rereading the one you made at the time. Compare them. You will see where a fact became harder to remember or a motive began to seem clearer than it actually was. Some of what changed may be understanding you earned. Some may be what time allowed you to forget. A dated record lets you be fair to the earlier person who made the decision with less information, while giving the person affected by it the benefit of what you know now.

## The Final Word

Your first account may explain why you acted. A fuller account can tell you what to do next.

## Closing questions

What would someone else have noticed in a disagreement you remember clearly?

Which past experience helps explain your reaction without settling whether it was fair?

Where would a correction have to reach for it to make a difference?
