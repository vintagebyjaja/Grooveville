# LAW 10 — STRATEGY

## Opening questions

What are you trying to change that you cannot decide on by yourself?

When you want people to help, what do you usually ask of them?

Which part of a long project do you tend to start with?

## Understanding the Law

Strategy means choosing a sequence of actions that advances a purpose under real conditions. Start by naming what you are trying to change, who can decide it and what each person needs to act. An effective sequence gives the next step somewhere to land.

## The Reality Behind It

A group asking for a safer crossing may need records from a transportation department, attendance from nearby residents and a decision from the body that controls funding. The Federal Highway Administration's Safe Transportation for Every Pedestrian materials describe several countermeasures and their intended applications. Evidence about a countermeasure helps frame a proposal. Local conditions and authority still matter.

### Distinguish a Goal From an Instrument

A goal such as increasing participation describes a change you want. A petition, a meeting or a letter is an instrument for pursuing it. Each can be done well and still fail to move a decision. If a workshop will enroll people only after its fees have been approved, an advertising campaign may bring more attention to the barrier without changing it. Ask which condition has to become true before people can take the next step. Choose an instrument that can help make it true.

A strategy can fail because a person working on it assumes everyone else values the same outcome. A supervisor may want more people trained. An employee may want paid time. A customer may want shorter delays. Those interests could support the same change for different reasons. State which part of the plan meets each interest and which conflicts remain. If you hide the cost from the person who will eventually have to pay it, their support can disappear when it matters most.

Some people will support a failure if supporting it preserves a relationship or puts them on the visible side of an argument. Make room for somebody to say the plan is wrong for a reason they can explain. Give dissent a time and a place to matter. If the group is still collecting information, name what evidence could change the request. If a commitment is already made, tell people what is open and what now requires a new decision.

## Where We Go Wrong

We sometimes confuse visibility with progress. A packed meeting can show support without identifying who has the authority to change the intersection. We can also become so focused on winning a decision that we promise allies benefits we cannot deliver. Strategy respects informed consent. Manipulation relies on people making choices they would reconsider if they knew what you knew.

### Give Each Step a Job

Imagine you want your employer to offer paid entry-level training. Separate people you need to consult from people who can make the decision. Supervisors can explain the training needed. Workers can identify which hours they can attend and what an unpaid requirement would cost. The person controlling the budget can explain how a new expense is considered. If you invite all of them to approve the idea at one meeting, you may get enthusiasm and no path to a decision.

Build the work around a result you can describe. “Offer three paid training shifts before the next hiring cycle” is something people can cost and discuss. If the request is too big to decide now, ask what evidence would support one trial. You may need a record of the current practice, an estimate of staff time or a partner who can teach a particular skill. Assign these tasks and verify that the people taking them have agreed.

Power changes some strategic choices. People relying on a supervisor for shifts may have good reason to raise an issue together or through a representative. People with more security can submit a proposal and take responsibility for keeping the answer visible. If a leader asks you to lend your name to a plan, find out what you are agreeing to and whether you can withdraw. Effective cooperation leaves each person with enough information to make a real choice.

Plans get stale when they are held together by loyalty to the first person who proposed them. Create a point at which the group decides whether to continue, change its request or stop. Review what the sequence accomplished and which assumptions failed. Honest records allow somebody who joins later to pick up the work without having to inherit a feud.

## Applying the Law

Write a specific outcome and the name or office that can approve it. Work backward. What information would that office consider? Who has standing or experience that should inform the proposal? What would change your mind? Set a date for the next decision and assign preparation people can agree to. Keep a simple account of what each action achieved.

### Build a Case People Can Examine

A group proposing a change needs evidence that fits the institution's decision and evidence that helps it understand the human consequences. For a street crossing, a traffic count can answer one question. A walk with parents, older residents or disabled neighbors can answer another. When the two accounts differ, make the difference visible. A crossing may meet a technical threshold while being too difficult to use at the time students arrive. Name what each kind of evidence establishes rather than asking one to stand in for the other.

Decide which people need access to the evidence before the meeting. An evening hearing can exclude people who work nights. A document full of technical terms can exclude residents who have a relevant observation but little reason to know the terminology. A strategy can include distributing a short explanation, offering another way to comment and checking that objections are accurately recorded. None of those choices guarantees agreement. They improve the chance that disagreement concerns the proposed action.

Accountability gives a victory a future. If a committee approves a trial, know who has to schedule it, who has money for the work and when people will see the result. Agree on what would count as improvement and what could expose an unintended cost. A pilot that continues forever without review can turn a temporary decision into a permanent arrangement nobody chose. A pilot with a public review date keeps the decision where people can see it.

There are reasons to compromise and reasons to wait. If a concession moves you closer to the purpose, explain it to the people supporting the work and document what was agreed. If the concession shifts a serious cost to people who had no say, put the question back before them. A persuasive strategy respects the difference between having the votes and having earned the trust on which you will rely next time.

## The Community Connection

Invite people affected by a decision to help define the demand. They may identify a concern your initial plan overlooked. A route used by someone in a wheelchair or by children walking from a bus stop may reveal a different priority from one seen in a daytime drive-by.

### Read the Timing and Leave a Trail

An organization may consider an expense once a year or change practice at the beginning of a school term. A proposal may be right and arrive after the money has been committed. Find when the next decision is open, what earlier milestones shape it and who writes the first version. A group that knows the calendar can ask for evidence or participation before deliberation reaches the stage where choices are difficult to change.

Keep records a replacement can use. A new member should be able to see what the group asked for, why it made that request, who responded and what remains unresolved. Preserve the evidence separately from the group's interpretation. If circumstances change, the next person can review the original account and decide whether the same strategy still fits. This is especially useful in organizations where the people who start a project are rarely the ones who see it finish.

A delay can give you time to learn or time to lose momentum. Ask what useful thing a group can do before the next formal decision. It might collect two pieces of missing information, take part in a related decision or build a working relationship with someone affected. If the delay is open-ended, set a review date. Continued effort belongs to people with other commitments, and they deserve to know why you are asking them to keep giving it.

If your plan succeeds, check who can use the result. An employer might add paid training and still require workers to attend outside the hours they can afford. A city might add a crossing and leave the connecting route inaccessible. A group's achievement should be visible in the choices it creates. When the result fails at that point, ask which part of the decision can still be changed and who should help shape it.

### Use Influence While Making It Open to Challenge

A person with an established reputation often has the ear of a decision maker. That access can help a group understand how a process works. It can also allow a few people to decide what they will ask for before anybody else can speak. If you have an informal conversation about a shared issue, tell the group what you learned and which commitments you made. Give others a chance to correct what you thought you heard. Make sure your relationship with the person in authority does not become the only way the group can reach them.

A strategy often includes negotiation over things with different value to different parties. Suppose a program wants earlier access to a public room, while the office responsible for it needs someone to meet an opening requirement. That concern may be addressed by training an additional volunteer or moving only one block of the schedule. Ask what the other side needs to accomplish and whether providing it would change the decision. You can be flexible on the method while remaining clear about the outcome that matters to participants.

In public debates, delays can be a way of avoiding a decision. They can also allow people to find information or give notice to groups that could not respond before. Ask what action the delay permits and when the information will be heard. Agree on who will do the work in the interval. If nobody will say when a matter will return, you may need a request that asks directly for a date or an account of how to put the item back on the agenda.

Build more than one path where possible. A request for new funding might fail while a narrower change in procedure remains available. A local organization may be able to support a pilot that gives a larger institution a chance to learn from it. A second path should have a purpose of its own and an honest account of what it will leave unresolved. Sometimes a small change reaches fewer people but can be made sooner. Sometimes the group may decide that taking the smaller step would make it harder to secure a fairer decision later.

When your influence depends on relationships, be careful of the obligations a private agreement can put on others. Tell a potential ally what you are asking and why. If you disagree about a decision, take the strongest version of their concern back to the group. Strategy can be firm about interests and still allow people to choose whether they want to work together again.

Let an unsuccessful effort leave something useful behind. If a vote fails, ask whether the objection was cost, jurisdiction, timing or a disagreement about the purpose. Record which people offered reasons and which responded without committing. Give the group a chance to decide whether pursuing another route is worth its time. The result can be disappointing and still tell you where the earlier plan depended on support that never reached the point of decision.

## The Final Word

A strategy is ready to use when the people doing the work understand where their part leads.

## Closing questions

Which step would make more sense if you worked backward from the decision?

Who should have a say before you ask others to support your plan?

What would tell you that your effort is moving things forward?
