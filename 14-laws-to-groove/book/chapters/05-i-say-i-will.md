# LAW 05 — I SAY = I WILL

## The Law

Treat your word as something another person may use to plan their day.

## The Story

Fictional illustration.

Rafael agreed to open the food pantry on Thursday because the question came while everybody was putting on coats. He had the key. He had opened before. “I got it,” he said.

On Wednesday his employer asked him to cover a shift that he needed to take. He saw the pantry reminder and meant to call after he confirmed the hours. By the time he had both answers it was late enough that he felt awkward calling. He texted the coordinator on Thursday morning, on his way into work.

At ten, a woman with two children found the pantry locked. She called the number on the sign and was told to wait. She could spare twenty minutes before her parking ran out. Across town, the coordinator left a dentist's office with a numb cheek and drove over with her key. She opened forty minutes late. The woman had gone.

The next time Rafael saw the coordinator, Janice, he told her he was sorry. He began to explain about his check. She told him she understood why he'd taken the shift. What she wanted to know was what time on Wednesday he had realized she needed a new opener.

They sat down with the volunteer calendar. Rafael could take the two Thursdays that followed his regular days off. On both nights he parked near the pantry and put the key in the bag that went to work with him. The other Thursdays went on the calendar as unfilled. Janice had taken to saying “we'll find somebody” in front of the group, then quietly finding herself. Seeing the blank spaces made it possible for the volunteers to talk about covering them.

## The Reality

Commitments move through a chain. When you agree to bring a ladder, someone else may decline to borrow one. When a landlord promises to be there at nine, a tenant may give up paid hours to meet them. A vague promise can tie up real resources, especially when the person waiting has little control over the schedule.

A promise about your own action is usually easier to keep than a promise that depends on another person. You can commit to submitting a form by Friday. If somebody else approves it, you can explain the approval timeline and agree on when you will check back. Making that distinction early can save the other person from trying to decipher silence.

## The Lesson

Rafael had good reason to want both commitments to work out. By leaving the pantry promise unsettled, he made it harder for anybody else to solve. Early notice can be useful even when you are still waiting on an answer. On Wednesday he could have said when the conflict became possible, when he expected to know more, and where the spare key was.

A group has duties here as well. If it punishes every honest refusal, it is likely to hear too many yeses. People who coordinate work can make it easier to decline by asking who is available, recording unfilled assignments and planning for cancellations. Sometimes the most honest sentence a volunteer can offer is “I can come after two.” A calendar can use that sentence.

## The Groove

Review your recent promises and find one you made without checking the time, cost or other people's availability. Make the commitment precise or revise it while the other person can still act. Include a time for your next update when the outcome depends on somebody else. After a missed commitment, ask what the other person lost and offer a repair you can actually make.

## The Final Word

Rafael's name became dependable on the calendar once the calendar showed the days he could really give.
