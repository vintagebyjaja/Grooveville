# LAW 05 — I SAY = I WILL

## Opening questions

What makes it easy or hard for you to say no?

Which promise are other people counting on you to keep this week?

How do you usually respond when you realize you might be late or have to cancel?

## Understanding the Law

Your word has value because another person can use it to plan. I Say = I Will treats reliability as a practice with three parts: give a promise you can carry, communicate when its conditions change, and repair the cost when you fall short.

### Pay Attention to What People Build Around You

Dependability is often easiest to see when it fails. If a parent promises to come at four, another adult may take a different shift. If a contractor promises that water will be restored before dinner, a tenant may decide whether to take children somewhere else. The person waiting can spend time, money or goodwill they cannot immediately recover. When a commitment falls apart, their first concern may be the choice they already made on the strength of your word.

A person can be sincere and still unreliable. A leader may genuinely value rest and routinely send assignments late in the evening. A friend may fully intend to repay a small loan, then promise the same paycheck to several people. A definition based on perceived alignment reminds us to pay attention to the experience of people who rely on the behavior. They have a right to compare what they were told with what happened. They also have incomplete information about constraints. Explain what matters for the next decision without offering a personal account they did not ask to receive.

Be deliberate about what changes when you use the word guarantee. In ordinary conversation, “I’ll try” may tell an experienced listener that the job still needs a backup. A newcomer may hear it as agreement if no one says who will follow up. Ask what the other person understood. A plain recap in writing can be useful for work, shared expenses and community commitments. The form need only match what is at risk. A brief text confirming the time of a pickup can be enough.

## The Reality Behind It

A commitment can set off other decisions. A parent who has been promised a pickup may accept a shift. A volunteer coordinator who has an opener can stop looking for one. Management researchers Tony Simons and colleagues, in their 2022 review, describe behavioral integrity as perceived alignment between words and deeds and review research connecting that perception with outcomes including trust. The evidence explains a pattern in organizational settings. It does not establish that a broken promise always has the same meaning.

### Distinguish a Favor From an Arrangement

When a relative agrees to keep an eye on a child for twenty minutes, they may be offering an exception. If you start relying on the same favor every week, ask whether they are willing to make it a recurring arrangement. Ask what happens on weeks they cannot do it. Give them a chance to tell you what it has been costing them. Some obligations can be traded or shared. Others need payment, a new schedule or a second person.

Misunderstandings often begin when one person hears an agreement and the other remembers a conversation. A friend who says they can help you move may mean they could lift a few boxes. You may have heard that they have a van and an entire morning. Settle what is needed, what is available and when you will confirm. For an important obligation, the account should be accessible later. Agreeing in writing is a way to preserve the terms, even when people trust each other.

There is value in leaving room to say a commitment is over. A plan that depended on a neighbor being home at four should change when the neighbor starts a new job. Ask how much notice the change requires, whether responsibilities can be handed off and what remains owed from the previous arrangement. If there was no conversation about ending it, have one before either party feels they have to disappear to be free of it.

An agreement can be clear and still unfair. Someone who needs help may accept conditions that a person with more options would decline. When you are the one who sets the terms, consider whether the other person can realistically refuse. Where a role is optional, say that plainly. Where a role is a job, distinguish the work that is required from favors you are asking somebody to consider.

## Where We Go Wrong

A generous impulse can lead to an unrealistic yes. So can an organization that treats a refusal as disloyalty. The damage grows when a person knows about a conflict but waits until they can offer a complete solution. An early, uncertain update often leaves the other person more options than a definite answer that arrives too late.

### A Promise Has a Recipient

Start by understanding what the other person needs the promise to do. If they ask you to attend a meeting, perhaps it matters that you hear the discussion. Perhaps they need a vote and your absence would change the result. You can agree to attend and still miss the actual need. Check whether a remote call, a written response or arrival at a certain time would meet it. A smaller, accurate promise may serve better than a large promise defined by guesswork.

In a joint project, name the things outside your control. You may own the draft while someone else owns the approval. If you say the report will go out Wednesday and the approver has never seen it, you created a deadline another person cannot use. Describe what you can deliver and when you will know enough to make the next promise. Keep that intermediate promise even if it lets you say only what is still outstanding.

Sometimes two good commitments collide. The easiest person to ask to wait may be a person who has accommodated you before. That availability can hide the costs they keep absorbing. If you have to choose, make the choice explicitly. Notify the affected person, offer what can be repaired and learn what would make another conflict less likely. A parent who has missed several pickup times may need a ruling about which shift they can accept, not a more elaborate apology.

The ability to say no is unevenly distributed. A worker can risk a penalty for refusing extra hours. A junior volunteer may fear losing a reference for declining. People with authority can change the cost of telling the truth. Ask for actual availability, build a backup and stop framing structural understaffing as an individual character test.

## Applying the Law

Say what you will do, when you will do it and which part depends on someone else. “I'll submit it Friday and check on Tuesday” is a commitment you can track. If new information makes a promise uncertain, send the information while it can still change the other person's plans. After a miss, ask what they lost. Offer a repair that addresses an actual cost.

### Repair the Cost and Change the Pattern

Start a repair with the action you took or failed to take. If you canceled late and another person paid a fee, say you understand when they found out and what they had to do. If you can cover the charge or split it, discuss it. If the loss was time, ask whether there is a practical way to return some of it. Sometimes an explanation will matter. It describes why the commitment broke, while the affected person can still decide how much trust to place in the next one.

A group can make its promises visible. Write down which hours are covered, which duties need a backup, and which services depend on enough people showing up. Do not claim all hours are covered because everybody said they would try. That makes the resulting failure look like an individual problem when the group accepted an unresolved schedule. Where paid coverage or a smaller service is the only reliable option, give those choices a fair hearing.

Someone can earn a new opportunity after being unreliable. Offer a task that can be fulfilled under conditions they can explain. Learn how they will tell you if those conditions change. When a promise goes well, give that evidence the weight it deserves. A group that keeps a permanent account of failures and treats every successful repair as ordinary has decided more about the future than its evidence warrants.

## The Community Connection

Make incomplete coverage visible in a group calendar. Allow people to say which hours they can give, and arrange who steps in when somebody cancels. A schedule that publicly thanks people for every yes and privately makes one person fill the gaps will give the group an inaccurate account of its own capacity.

### Remember Who Will Carry the Next Decision

At a school event, the group can put commitments on one page and check the steps that three other tasks depend on. The person with the room reservation needs enough information to confirm the time. The person who can authorize an expense needs to know its size and date. A truthful statement such as “we have one driver and need another” gives the organization a chance to change the plan before someone else has to rescue it.

At work, a leader's commitment often takes the form of a standard. If they say workers can report a problem without losing a shift, the procedure needs a way to notice whether that promise is being kept. Check who reports, how assignments are recorded and who can review a disputed change. There may be reasons a schedule changed. A trustworthy process allows that explanation to be examined. People should not have to rely entirely on the same authority that made the promise.

Reliability grows through an honest account of what you can do now. It may take a while to rebuild it after people have made decisions around inaccurate information. You can start with a smaller commitment that you are ready to keep, communicate on time and let the people affected draw their own conclusions. The aim is to give others solid information when they have to decide.

### Keep a Record Where It Can Help Someone

When the work crosses a shift or changes hands, an agreement belongs where both people can see it. A family member collecting a prescription may need the correct location and the last time the pharmacy will hold it. A coworker expecting a repair may need to know which part has been ordered and how to tell when it arrives. A vague note can create the comforting impression that help is coming while leaving the next person to decide what to do.

Use a form that serves the people who have to act. A shared notebook can work for adults who keep different hours. A short message can be enough for a one-time exchange. For a job that involves money or several participants, use a record that identifies who agreed to the action, what it costs and when an update is expected. Ask the person receiving it whether it contains what they need. Add a phone number or contact role when someone may need to ask a question after you leave.

Written promises can become burdensome if a group requires elaborate records for work that is quick to confirm. Spend more effort on commitments that carry significant costs or where repeated confusion has been expensive. A record should make it easier to fulfill the promise or address a failure. If keeping the record takes more time than the work, ask which detail actually matters.

There is a limit to the value of a promise made to win time. A customer who hears that an order will be fixed may give up the chance to arrange a replacement. If you cannot tell whether you can deliver, say what remains uncertain. Give them permission to make a different arrangement without waiting for your preferred outcome. Respect for the person receiving a commitment includes respect for the options they still have.

Reliable people also need room to change what they offer as their lives change. A person can explain that a regular commitment is becoming hard to meet, ask what needs to happen before it ends and make the handoff while they can still be useful. That is one way to leave a group stronger than you found it.

## The Final Word

Give your word at the size you can deliver it.

## Closing questions

Which promise could you make clearer before someone builds a plan around it?

What would an honest early update sound like if you still had no solution?

When you have let someone down, what might repair part of what they lost?
