# The 14 Laws to Groove — Educator & Community Discussion Guide

Companion to The 14 Laws to Groove by Jabari Jaja

## High school

Read one law together and underline its claim, source and proposed action in different colors. Model how to distinguish an observation from an inference. Use imaginary or public examples for exercises involving money, family or mental health. Provide a short source excerpt before assigning an essay and give students time to explain evidence in their own words.

## College

Pair each law with its cited source or method. Ask students to examine the sample, definitions and limits, then locate and evaluate a second source. Have students develop a claim and counterclaim before arguing a position. Assess their use of evidence, attention to alternative explanations and ability to connect the argument to institutional decisions.

## Adult community groups

Invite participants to name a shared problem and select a relevant law. Keep personal disclosure optional and let people use hypothetical or public examples. Use the application exercise to draft a concrete change and agree who will try it and when the group will revisit it.

## Facilitation and assessment

Give participants a way to pass or contribute in writing. Assess reasoning through a clear claim, relevant evidence, a fair account of another position, and a conclusion that follows from the evidence. Distinguish practical advice from research findings. When studying a sensitive subject, avoid asking participants to diagnose one another, share financial records, or disclose private experiences.


# LAW 01 — REFLECTION

## Learning objectives

Separate observation from inference. Explain how decision-making authority shapes the consequences of an error. Evaluate what a hiring audit measures.

## Key terms

Observation is an account of what could be recorded. Inference is an interpretation of that account. An audit experiment varies one feature across comparable cases to measure a difference in treatment.

## Discussion questions

1. What detail makes an interpretation reasonable without making it certain?

2. How can earlier mistreatment shape a fair response to a new situation?

3. When should an immediate action and a formal finding require different standards of evidence?

4. What does a callback reveal about hiring?

5. Which repair fits a mistake that entered a permanent record?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Defend or challenge the use of a review period before recording workplace misconduct.

2. Argue what an employer should do when an audit detects unequal callbacks but cannot establish individual intent.

## Research paper prompt

Compare the Bertrand and Mullainathan experiment with a later hiring audit. Explain differences in setting, design and the conclusions each permits.

## Real-world application

Use a routine dispute about a missed handoff. Write what could be verified, what you inferred and one question that could change your interpretation.

## Suggested sources

Bertrand, Marianne, and Sendhil Mullainathan. 2004. “Are Emily and Greg More Employable Than Lakisha and Jamal? A Field Experiment on Labor Market Discrimination.” American Economic Review 94 (4), 991–1013. https://www.nber.org/papers/w9873

## Optional group activity

Assign pairs the same ambiguous incident. Give each pair a different verified detail and let them revise a draft record after comparing notes.

# LAW 02 — PHONE DOWN

## Learning objectives

Explain selective attention in conversation. Assess when device restrictions help discussion and when they reduce participation.

## Key terms

Selective attention prioritizes certain signals. An accessibility accommodation removes a barrier to participation. Self-report records what participants say about their own experience.

## Discussion questions

1. When might checking a device count as attending to the person in front of you?

2. What should an urgent-call exception include?

3. Why might one person recall an exchange differently after several interruptions?

4. What does the restaurant experiment tell us about its own participants?

5. What can a teacher observe that an enjoyment survey cannot?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Defend a limited device policy for one classroom activity, including an accessibility provision.

2. Argue who should set expectations for phone use at family gatherings.

## Research paper prompt

Compare the restaurant experiment with a study of device use in another setting. Discuss how the outcomes were measured and whether the findings transfer.

## Real-world application

Plan a short conversation using an agreed interruption rule. Ask both participants what helped them follow the discussion.

## Suggested sources

Dwyer, Ryan J., Kostadin Kushlev, and Elizabeth W. Dunn. 2018. “Smartphone Use Undermines Enjoyment of Face-to-Face Social Interactions.” Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 78, 233–239. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2017.10.007

## Optional group activity

Give teams an accessibility, caregiving or ordinary meeting scenario. Compare the rules each team proposes.

# LAW 03 — SCOUT

## Learning objectives

Identify direct and indirect costs of entering a program. Compare institutional, participant and employer accounts.

## Key terms

A credential attests to knowledge, training or skills under specified terms. An indirect cost results from participation and may be absent from the advertised price. Verification checks a claim against an independent source or record.

## Discussion questions

1. How would you assess a credential employers describe inconsistently?

2. When might a weaker program be a practical choice for a participant?

3. Who benefits when entry requirements are kept informal?

4. Which questions can a recruiter credibly answer?

5. How should you weigh one participant's account against program data?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Argue for a standard disclosure document for vocational programs.

2. Defend or challenge the idea that program schedules should be weighed alongside completion rates.

## Research paper prompt

Compare two documented routes into the same occupation. Analyze entry requirements, pay, recognized credentials and barriers involving time or travel.

## Real-world application

Use public materials to compare two training options. Record one fact, estimate and unresolved question for each.

## Suggested sources

U.S. Department of Labor. “What Is a Registered Apprenticeship Program?” Apprenticeship.gov. https://www.apprenticeship.gov/employers/registered-apprenticeship-program

## Optional group activity

Role-play a program adviser, current apprentice and applicant with a night shift. Have each person identify information missing from a sample brochure.

# LAW 04 — BRANDING

## Learning objectives

Distinguish character from reputation and stereotyping. Evaluate the evidence used in an assessment of performance.

## Key terms

Character is the pattern of choices you make. Reputation is how others describe you. A stereotype assigns traits to an individual based on assumptions about a group.

## Discussion questions

1. Which features of a reputation can a person influence?

2. When does advice about presentation put the burden of bias on the person judged?

3. How can self-respect appear without a confident public performance?

4. What evidence should support a label such as reliable or aggressive?

5. Who should be able to challenge a written assessment?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Argue whether managers should remove personality adjectives from evaluations.

2. Evaluate whether a blind review can solve the problems of bias in a hiring stage you choose.

## Research paper prompt

Locate an agency guidance document and an empirical study of workplace evaluations. Compare their purposes, evidence and limits.

## Real-world application

Rewrite a fictional performance review using observable job behavior. Identify which remaining judgment needs more evidence.

## Suggested sources

U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. “Section 15 Race and Color Discrimination.” Compliance Manual. https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/section-15-race-and-color-discrimination

Bertrand, Marianne, and Sendhil Mullainathan. 2004. “Are Emily and Greg More Employable Than Lakisha and Jamal? A Field Experiment on Labor Market Discrimination.” American Economic Review 94 (4), 991–1013. https://www.nber.org/papers/w9873

## Optional group activity

Have two groups score the same anonymous work sample with different criteria. Compare results and jointly revise the criteria.

# LAW 05 — I SAY = I WILL

## Learning objectives

Explain behavioral integrity and reliance. Identify early notice and repair practices for a missed promise.

## Key terms

Behavioral integrity is perceived alignment between words and deeds. Reliance occurs when somebody acts in expectation that a commitment will be kept. Repair addresses the effects of a broken commitment.

## Discussion questions

1. When does a generous yes create more difficulty than a clear no?

2. How early should a person disclose a possible conflict?

3. What can a group do with a promise that depends on a third party?

4. How should one missed promise affect future trust?

5. Who does unpaid recovery work when volunteers cancel?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Defend or challenge a notice requirement for volunteer shifts.

2. Argue whether a small organization should guarantee backup coverage before advertising public hours.

## Research paper prompt

Compare research on behavioral integrity with a study of scheduling or trust. Explain the kinds of outcomes each measures.

## Real-world application

Rewrite a vague commitment to include an action, date and a next update. Exchange drafts and mark any remaining ambiguities.

## Suggested sources

Simons, Tony, Hannes Leroy, and Lisa H. Nishii. 2022. “Revisiting Behavioral Integrity: Progress and New Directions After 20 Years.” Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior 9, 365–389. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-012420-062016

## Optional group activity

Model a missed opening shift with a paper calendar. Trace the effect of the same cancellation at three notice intervals.

# LAW 06 — PEERS AREN'T DEARS

## Learning objectives

Identify degrading behavior, a breach of confidentiality and a workable boundary. Explain what bystanders can do.

## Key terms

A boundary identifies conduct you will accept and how you will respond. Confidentiality is an obligation to protect information shared in trust. A bystander witnesses conduct without being its direct target.

## Discussion questions

1. How can you assess teasing when the person affected laughed?

2. What distinguishes public accountability from more humiliation?

3. What does the person who received a private disclosure owe the speaker?

4. When might distancing yourself be more useful than another conversation?

5. How can a bystander help without taking control of the response?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Defend or challenge a rule requiring groups to address derogatory jokes when no one complains.

2. Argue when private mediation is appropriate after a breach of trust.

## Research paper prompt

Compare a written school bullying policy with public health definitions. Assess how it handles isolated harm and recurring conduct.

## Real-world application

Write a boundary and a respectful response to it for a fictional peer dispute. Avoid repeating private details.

## Suggested sources

U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. “What Is Bullying.” StopBullying.gov. https://www.stopbullying.gov/bullying/what-is-bullying

## Optional group activity

Give small groups a fictional group-chat exchange. Ask them to write a bystander response and discuss its likely consequences.

# LAW 07 — ENDOWMENT

## Learning objectives

Compare financial and nonfinancial resources. Interpret redlining records and describe basic U.S. account and investment choices.

## Key terms

Endowment is a resource received or developed that can create an opportunity. A budget plans inflows and outflows. A 401(k) is a U.S. employer-sponsored retirement plan. Diversification spreads exposure across investments.

## Discussion questions

1. What does a helpful contact make possible that cash alone might not?

2. What do historical maps show about the assumptions of federal appraisers?

3. How could two households with the same income have different savings options?

4. Which money belongs within easy reach, and why?

5. Who should teach people about investment fees and risk?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Argue whether schools should require a financial literacy course or embed the material across subjects.

2. Make a case for one community response to unequal access to inherited resources, including a serious objection.

## Research paper prompt

Examine a historical area description and later local housing records. Explain which inferences each source supports and where the chain of evidence stops.

## Real-world application

Use fictional figures to prepare a pay-date budget, assign transactions to checking or savings, and compare two sample retirement plan fees.

## Suggested sources

Digital Scholarship Lab, University of Richmond, and partners. Mapping Inequality: Redlining in New Deal America. https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/redlining/

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. “An Essential Guide to Building an Emergency Fund.” https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/

Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator. https://edie.fdic.gov/

Internal Revenue Service. “401(k) Plans.” https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/401k-plans

U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. “Asset Allocation, Diversification, and Rebalancing.” Investor.gov. https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/asset-allocation

## Optional group activity

Give teams different sets of household resources and identical incomes. Compare the opportunities they can realistically accept.

# LAW 08 — RISKY REWARDS

## Learning objectives

Distinguish downside, reversibility and risk tolerance. Identify who bears the costs of a decision.

## Key terms

Downside is an adverse outcome and its consequences. Reversibility describes how readily a decision can be undone. Risk tolerance is willingness to accept uncertain results or losses.

## Discussion questions

1. What can a smaller trial establish?

2. How would a deadline change the cost of waiting?

3. When does one person's career risk become a household risk?

4. Which expenses count as essentials during an interruption in income?

5. How can a grant change a program's access without changing its curriculum?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Argue for or against paying a stipend during required training.

2. Defend a rule for deciding when an organization should pilot a risky proposal.

## Research paper prompt

Compare data on financial buffers with research on participation in a workforce program. Identify plausible links and the limits of causal inference.

## Real-world application

Evaluate a fictional job change using favorable, ordinary and adverse scenarios. Set a stopping rule and identify who must agree.

## Suggested sources

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. “An Essential Guide to Building an Emergency Fund.” https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/

## Optional group activity

Have groups compare the same opportunity for two fictional households with different expenses, savings and caregiving obligations.

# LAW 09 — DISENGAGEMENT

## Learning objectives

Distinguish a pause from abandonment and explain when safety changes the decision.

## Key terms

De-escalation reduces the intensity of conflict. A planned pause sets a condition or time for resuming ordinary discussion. Safety planning tailors practical steps to particular dangers and needs.

## Discussion questions

1. When should a person offer a time to resume a disagreement?

2. How can someone disengage while preserving a way to report harm?

3. Why might announcing a departure be unsafe in some situations?

4. What practical help can a friend offer without taking over?

5. What obligations remain after a committee chair ends a heated meeting?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Argue for a right to pause a facilitated dispute and specify how to prevent misuse.

2. Defend an independent reporting channel when an institutional authority is involved in a conflict.

## Research paper prompt

Compare two public conflict-response protocols. Assess who can initiate a pause, what is documented and how questions of safety are addressed.

## Real-world application

Write a pause-and-return message for a fictional scheduling disagreement. Identify when a different response would be needed.

## Suggested sources

National Domestic Violence Hotline. “Create Your Personal Safety Plan.” https://www.thehotline.org/plan-for-safety/

## Optional group activity

Role-play a low-stakes committee dispute with a neutral chair. Compare a timed pause and immediate continuation.

# LAW 10 — STRATEGY

## Learning objectives

Map decision authority. Sequence steps and assess the role of transparency and consent.

## Key terms

Decision authority is the power to approve a specified outcome. A stakeholder is a person or group affected by a decision. A decision point is the moment a choice changes what can happen next.

## Discussion questions

1. Who can approve a change in a public setting you know?

2. How do you distinguish gathering support from achieving a decision?

3. When can publication of a plan create pressure without informed consent?

4. What makes local experience relevant evidence?

5. Which milestones can show that a campaign is changing its prospects?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Argue how a public agency should weigh resident testimony when technical measures point elsewhere.

2. Defend a specific stakeholder participation rule for a small community project.

## Research paper prompt

Trace a public project from proposal through decision. Compare the agency's criteria with community claims and track which influenced the outcome.

## Real-world application

Map who controls a single local decision and what information would allow them to act. Draft a request people affected could review.

## Suggested sources

Federal Highway Administration. “Safe Transportation for Every Pedestrian.” https://highways.dot.gov/safety/pedestrian-bicyclist/step

## Optional group activity

Assign roles in a crossing proposal and negotiate what the group will ask for after hearing each role's evidence.

# LAW 11 — TIME

## Learning objectives

Estimate task and transition time. Explain opportunity cost and assess unequal scheduling control.

## Key terms

Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative forgone. A time buffer reserves minutes for uncertainty. Scheduling control is a person's ability to decide or reliably know when obligations occur.

## Discussion questions

1. Which parts of a calendar often go unrecorded?

2. Who gets to value their own time in a meeting?

3. What can a time-use diary measure about overlapping care?

4. What distinguishes a difficult task from one with an undefined next step?

5. How should people communicate a delay when they do not know its length?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Argue for or against advance notice requirements for employee schedules.

2. Defend a change in school scheduling using evidence about obligations outside school.

## Research paper prompt

Use American Time Use Survey materials to compare time spent in two activities. Discuss the survey method and which tasks it might understate.

## Real-world application

Construct a realistic weekday from a fictional schedule. Include travel, sleep, care and time buffers, then revise it after a missed bus.

## Suggested sources

U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. American Time Use Survey. https://www.bls.gov/tus/

## Optional group activity

Have groups redesign a standing meeting for people with different work and care schedules.

# LAW 12 — PLANNING

## Learning objectives

Explain the 12 Ps and identify dependencies, contingencies and accountable handoffs.

## Key terms

A dependency is a condition a later task relies on. A contingency is a prepared response to a meaningful disruption. A handoff transfers work or information from one person to another.

## Discussion questions

1. What makes a date different from an executable plan?

2. How can an unconfirmed assumption affect people outside a project?

3. When does planning become too elaborate to use?

4. Who should be authorized to activate a backup?

5. How can teams learn from a failure without assigning every consequence to one person?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Argue whether small grant recipients should submit formal contingency plans.

2. Defend a way to distribute responsibility when multiple people control a critical handoff.

## Research paper prompt

Compare a documented project's initial schedule and later reports. Identify dependencies and assess whether the records show what caused delays.

## Real-world application

Build a two-week plan for a fictional event. Assign ownership, verify premises and set a trigger for one backup.

## Suggested sources

U.S. Government Accountability Office. 2015. Schedule Assessment Guide: Best Practices for Project Schedules. GAO-16-89G. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-16-89g

## Optional group activity

Hand each team a last-minute problem with an event. Compare which prior decisions let them respond with least disruption.

# LAW 13 — MOTHER'S CHILD

## Learning objectives

Distinguish authenticity from performing a fixed identity. Analyze how roles, family and culture influence personal narrative.

## Key terms

Narrative identity is the evolving account through which someone connects life experiences with a possible future. Authenticity is acting with fidelity to one's considered values and history. A social role carries expectations connected to a position.

## Discussion questions

1. When can changing your presentation preserve something important about you?

2. How can family memories inform a story without owning it?

3. What can a title change, and what remains a matter of character?

4. Which information can a person reasonably keep private?

5. How can a community invite returning members to share what they learned without imposing a role?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Argue whether authenticity should be treated as an evaluation criterion in professional life.

2. Defend or challenge the view that reinvention can honor one's upbringing.

## Research paper prompt

Compare two public autobiographical accounts using narrative identity concepts. Consider how the authors' cultural and economic circumstances affect what they emphasize.

## Real-world application

Use a public speech or fictional biography to identify three values and one action that tests each. Personal disclosure is optional.

## Suggested sources

McAdams, Dan P., and Kate C. McLean. 2013. “Narrative Identity.” Current Directions in Psychological Science 22 (3), 233–238. https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721413475622

## Optional group activity

Compare how three written introductions describe the same fictional person. Discuss what the titles and the descriptions leave out.

# LAW 14 — MENTAL AWARENESS

## Learning objectives

Identify signals of strain and resources for support. Connect individual well-being with institutional conditions.

## Key terms

Stress is a response to demands or pressures. Emotional awareness is noticing and naming feelings and how they affect action. A barrier to care is a condition that makes suitable help harder to obtain.

## Discussion questions

1. How can someone recognize strain without diagnosing themselves or others?

2. What might a colleague be able to offer within appropriate boundaries?

3. How does privacy affect whether people use support?

4. When might a scheduling change do more than an individual coping strategy?

5. How can a group notice the work of caregiving when attendance slips?

## Argumentative essay prompts

1. Argue how a school should balance staff training on mental health with access to qualified providers.

2. Defend a workplace change aimed at reducing a specific source of strain.

## Research paper prompt

Compare two public reports on access to mental health services. Analyze what their measures show about cost, language or distance and where evidence is missing.

## Real-world application

Create a resource map for a fictional resident that includes routine practical support and qualified local services. Verify hours and eligibility using public records.

## Suggested sources

National Institute of Mental Health. “Caring for Your Mental Health.” https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/caring-for-your-mental-health

## Optional group activity

Use a fictional scenario to decide which support belongs with a friend, an organization or a qualified professional.
